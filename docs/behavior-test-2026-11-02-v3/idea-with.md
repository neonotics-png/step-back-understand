# Step-back understanding: Kent Beck's test-driven development

Sources checked 2026-11-02. Every claim below is tied to a page or document that was fetched and read during this pass; where a primary text could not be reached, the sentence says so and names the secondhand route. Evidence classes follow the skill: `documented`, `composition`, `interpretive`, `unknown`.

## Subject and question

**Subject.** Test-driven development as Kent Beck formulated it in *Test-Driven Development: By Example* (Addison-Wesley, 2002/2003): the two rules ("Don't write a line of new code unless you first have a failing automated test" and "Eliminate duplication"), the red/green/refactor cycle, and the test-first order of work.

**Open question.** Where did the red-green-refactor loop and the write-the-test-first rule come from: which earlier practices, people, tools and texts supplied each part, and what did Beck add?

## The shaping sources, each in its own right

### 1. The tape-era method: "type in the expected output tape, then code until the actual matches"

What it is. In batch computing of the 1950s and 60s a program read an input tape (time cards, say) and wrote an output tape (paychecks). The checkout discipline Beck describes was: before writing the program, produce the expected output by hand from a real input, then program until the machine's output matches. This is test-first for a whole program, with the "test" being a hand-made expected-output file and the "assertion" a comparison of tapes.

Where it is attested. Beck's 2002 draft Acknowledgments: "Finally, to the unknown author of the book which I read as a weird 12-year-old that suggested you type in the expected output tape from a real input tape, then code until the actual results matched the expected result, thank you, thank you, thank you." His 2012 Quora answer (via Wikipedia and Martini) says the same: "The original description of TDD was in an ancient book about programming. It said you take the input tape, manually type in the output tape you expect, then program until the actual output tape matches the expected output", that after writing the first xUnit framework in Smalltalk he remembered the book and tried it, and that older programmers respond "Of course. How else could you program?", which is why he says "rediscovering". Orosz adds, from his interview with Beck, that the book was one of his father's (Doug Beck, a programmer) from the tape-to-tape era, and that when Beck first tried the trick he found it absurd (the test is guaranteed to fail) until "his programming anxiety disappeared".

The practice was real and older than the book. Larman and Basili, with Weinberg as their source, record that NASA's Project Mercury (begun 1958) "ran with very short (half-day) iterations that were time boxed. The development team conducted a technical review of all changes, and, interestingly, applied the Extreme Programming practice of test-first development, planning and writing tests before each micro-increment. They also practiced top-down development with stubs." Weinberg's personal communication in the same paper: "We were doing incremental development as early as 1957, in Los Angeles, under the direction of Bernie Dimsdale [at IBM's Service Bureau Corporation]. He was a colleague of John von Neumann, so perhaps he learned it there ... the technique used was, as far as I can tell, indistinguishable from XP." Weinberg told Bolton in 2008: "We didn't call those things by those names back then", "I learned it from Bernie Dimsdale, who learned it from von Neumann", and that testing was the opening topic of his first book (Leeds and Weinberg, *Computer Programming Fundamentals*, 1961). McCracken's 1957 textbook, per Bolton's find quoted by Martini, says "The first attack on the checkout problem may be made before coding is begun", that a hand-calculated check case is needed, and that it is "highly desirable that the 'customer' prepare the check case". Whether McCracken's or Leeds and Weinberg's book is the one Beck read is `unknown`; Beck himself has offered "Mongo bonus points for anyone who can refer me to the original book" and no one has collected.

Signature traits: expected output fixed before coding; a mechanical comparison decides done; the customer may author the check; the unit of work is the whole program run.

### 2. Smalltalk culture and Ward Cunningham

What it is. Beck's professional programming began in Smalltalk at Tektronix in the mid-1980s alongside Ward Cunningham (Orosz; XpRoots). The Smalltalk way, as Mike Beedle's XpRoots page describes it and Cunningham "concurs", bundled refactoring, pair development, rapid change, constant integration, iterative development and "constant testing"; Beedle's line is "XP is the Smalltalk way generalized to other environments". Beck, in the SE Radio episode (Hiranabe's summary), says Smalltalk had a habit of testing: make a small change, run it, look, but nothing was automated; his framework idea was to map the Smalltalk workspace onto objects (workspace becomes a class, variables become instance variables, snippets become methods) and have the machine do the "print the result and compare" step, which became the assertion. Cunningham's own concerns, in his XP Immersion talk and his 2003 remarks to Udell, were talking about things that do not exist yet (CRC cards pointing at an empty spot on the table, wiki links to pages not yet written) and plainness: "Really, test-first is just be clear about what you want, write it, and then see if that's what you got." WyCash, Cunningham's bond-portfolio system, is the source of the multi-currency Money example and of the "method, motive and opportunity" story that opens the book (2002 draft: "Tell the WyCash multi-currency story ... Ward invents Money and MoneyBag. At the end of the day, the system was working").

Signature traits: tiny change, immediate run, immediate look; evolutionary design grown "little-by-little"; naming and plain statement of intent; reference to not-yet-existing things as a design move.

### 3. SUnit and "Simple Smalltalk Testing: With Patterns"

What it is. Beck's short paper and three-class framework (TestCase, TestResult, TestSuite; fixtures via setUp; checks as blocks sent to should:). Its opening: "Smalltalk has suffered because it lacked a testing culture." Its stance: UI-script tests are too brittle, so write tests and checks directly in Smalltalk; "I recommend that developers write their own unit tests, one per class"; "I recommend that developers spend 25-50% of their time developing tests"; an independent tester writes integration tests; each test creates and discards its own data so tests do not interact; the framework is "not intended to be complete solutions, but rather a starting point." It is a cookbook of patterns (Fixture, Test Case, Check, Test Suite), in the pattern-language style Beck and Cunningham had been applying since 1987.

What the paper does not say. Nothing in the reproduced text tells the reader to write the test before the code. The paper is about developers owning automated tests at all; the ordering rule is absent.

Dating. Beck's Canon TDD post: "I wrote SUnit in 1994 for a consulting client," and he demonstrated TDD to Cunningham at OOPSLA in Austin in October 1995. In SE Radio he guessed "around 1992", unsure. Cambridge University Press lists the column as *Smalltalk Report*, October 1994. Wikipedia's "1989" is unsupported by any source read here and conflicts with Beck's own dating.

### 4. JUnit, Erich Gamma and "Test Infected"

What it is. JUnit was written by Beck and Gamma pair-programming, test-first, on a flight from Zurich to OOPSLA 1997 in Atlanta (Fowler; Beck in SE Radio per Hiranabe: Gamma heard Beck's OOPSLA testing talk, they sat down and wrote it test-first, "an interesting bootstrap problem"; Beck dates it 1997). The 1998 article "Test Infected" argues "Every programmer knows they should write tests for their code. Few do", recommends "code a little, test a little, code a little, test a little", "or even better, to write a test that won't run, then write the code that will make it run", and describes the GUI: "The bar is initially green but turns into red as soon as there is an unsuccessful test." Its closing: "Once you've been test infected, your attitude toward development is likely to change", "Part of being test infected is not being able to go home if your tests aren't 100%."

The colors. Fowler: at C3 the team colored the whole window red when a test failed and green when all passed; "JUnit introduced doing this as a progress bar, and thus added some new vocabulary to software developers." The TDD book itself uses the vocabulary throughout ("You are seeing the dreaded red bar. Our testing framework (JUnit, in this case) has run", "Now we get the green bar, fabled in song and story").

### 5. Extreme Programming and the C3 project

What it is. XP, assembled by Beck on the Chrysler C3 payroll project from 1996 and published in *Extreme Programming Explained* (1999), made test-first a named team practice. Beck to Josuttis, January 2000: "The one most important practice is testing. Testing and refactoring", "Certainly without the tests, you're dead", "test-first-coding is also the practice that is most useful whatever project culture you have", and a coding episode starts with a pair writing "the next automated test case for an engineering task." On provenance he said: "I am the one who put all the pieces together, and the one who coined the name"; "The testing strategy in XP comes from Christopher Glaser, a compiler writer I worked with at MasPar"; "The evolutionary design philosophy comes from my long-time colleague Ward Cunningham." Ron Jeffries' CodeUnitTestFirst steps on Ward's wiki (find out what to do; write a unit test for the smallest increment; run it; if it passes go back to step 1; fix the immediate problem, re-run) are the XP-era version of the loop, with no refactor step named. Bill Wake's 2001 "stoplight" (green, yellow, red, green) is another contemporary rendering, "inspired by watching Kent Beck apply the TDD approach", and again refactoring is only an aside.

## What this reveals about the subject

- The two things the user asked about have different ancestries. **Write-the-test-first** is the old part: it is the tape-era checkout rule, which Beck read as a child, forgot, and re-applied once SUnit gave him a way to write a test in seconds rather than by typing a whole output tape. His own word for his role, "rediscovering", is accurate on the evidence: the ordering rule is attested at Project Mercury (via Weinberg) and in 1957 textbook prose before Beck was born.
- **Red/green/refactor** is the new part, and it is a composite. "Red" and "green" are JUnit's bar (1997), itself a progress-bar version of the C3 team's red/green window. "Refactor" is the Smalltalk-tradition practice Beck and Cunningham had been doing and that Opdyke and Fowler had written up. The 2002 draft is where the three words are first put together as "Red/green/refactor. The TDDs mantra."; the 1998 "Test Infected" article has the test-first order and the colored bar but no refactor step, Jeffries' and Wake's XP-era loops mention refactoring only in passing, and the SUnit paper has neither the order nor the colors. On the sources read, the mantra as a three-step unit is Beck's 2002 formulation.
- The grain is what Beck changed. Every ancestor works at a bigger unit: a whole program against an output tape, a micro-increment at Mercury, a class with its tests in SUnit. TDD's unit is one test, one assertion, "a little test that doesn't work, perhaps doesn't even compile", and the book spends its Part III (Fake It, Triangulate, Obvious Implementation) on how to keep steps that small. That is also why he frames the practice around fear and feedback ("Test-driven development (TDD) is a way of managing fear during programming"), a motive that none of the older sources state.
- "Make the test work quickly, committing whatever sins necessary" is the permission that the older practice could not give: with a whole program against a tape there is nothing to fake. It exists because the test is tiny and the refactor step follows.

## What remains distinctive

- The step size and the explicit license to fake (Fake It, Triangulate), with duplication-removal as the engine of design. Nothing read here attributes these to anyone but Beck.
- The reframing of testing as a design and anxiety-management technique rather than a checkout technique.
- Not established by this pass: how much of the refactor step's placement in the loop came from Beck versus the Smalltalk group around him; Beck himself says the ideas cannot be sorted from Ward's.

## What you can now recognize

- When you read "write the test first" in Beck, you can hear the tape: expected output written by hand before the program exists, with "code until it matches" as the stopping rule. Any TDD text that treats the test as a specification the customer could write is channeling McCracken's "the customer prepare the check case" and Weinberg's era, whether or not the author knows it.
- When you see "red bar" and "green bar", you are seeing JUnit's 1997 progress bar and the C3 team's colored window, not a metaphor Beck invented for the book. Pre-2002 descriptions of the loop that lack "refactor" (Jeffries' steps, Wake's stoplight, "Test Infected") are the loop before the mantra, and you can date a description by whether the third word is there.
- When Beck says "committing whatever sins necessary", you can recognize the thing only a tiny test makes possible, and you can see why the SUnit paper, with its "one test per class", could not have said it.
- The Money example and the "end of the day, the system was working" story are WyCash and Ward; the book's Introduction is Beck telling his mentor's story before his own.

## Unresolved branches and consequential uncertainty

- The identity of the "ancient book". McCracken 1957 and Leeds and Weinberg 1961 are candidates by content; Beck has not confirmed either. This does not change the conclusion that the ordering rule predates him.
- Christopher Glaser's "testing strategy" at MasPar: credited by Beck in 2000 as the source of XP's testing strategy, but nothing read here says what it was or how it relates to test-first.
- The exact year of SUnit: Beck says 1994 (2023) and "around 1992" (2010); publication was October 1994; Wikipedia's 1989 is unsupported.
- Whether Beck ever stated in his own words that the names Red and Green were taken from JUnit's bar. The derivation is a critic's (Dangoor's) and is consistent with the text, but is labeled interpretive above for that reason.
- The refactoring lineage (Opdyke's thesis, Fowler's book, *Smalltalk Best Practice Patterns*) was not studied in its own right; the XpRoots page is the only source for "TDD derives from refactoring practices".
- Beck's Quora answer and *Extreme Programming Explained* (1999) were not read in the original.

## What changes for the current task

No task beyond the understanding itself. If this feeds a write-up, the one correction to a common telling is that "Beck invented TDD" and "Beck rediscovered TDD" are both half right: the test-first rule was rediscovered, the red/green/refactor mantra and the tiny-step patterns were new in 2002.
