# Step-back understanding: Khruangbin

Sources checked 2026-11-02. No audio was inspected. Every claim about how the band or any source sounds comes from a named written source, mostly the members' own words in interviews; where a link is only a comparison, the text says so.

**Subject and question.** Khruangbin (Mark Speer, guitar; Laura Lee Ochoa, bass; Donald "DJ" Johnson, drums), formed in Houston around 2009–2010 and recorded in a barn in Burton, Texas. Why does a band from Houston sound the way it does: reverb-heavy guitar, bass-led grooves, mostly instrumental songs?

## The explanation

The short version, in the band's own words, is that the sound is a Houston rhythm section playing breaks, with a bass player who learned her instrument from Jamaican dub records and a guitarist who treats the guitar as the singer and reverb as the room. The Thai and Iranian records they are famous for loving shaped what they reached for; the members' own accounts of their method point closer to home, to Jamaica by way of record collections and to Houston churches. Each link below is stated by a band member unless the text says it is a comparison. Taking the three traits in turn:

**The bass leads because the bass player's whole musical education was dub, and because the writing order copies a dub mix.** Laura Lee had no training on bass; she was a third-grade maths teacher who had learned a little piano with Speer, and he "told me to follow the bass line" (Premier Guitar, 2024). What she then learned on was dub reggae, which she calls "my ABCs of music": Barrington Levy's *Robin Hood*, with "When Friday Comes" among the first bass lines she learned, and Scientist's *Scientist Wins the World Cup* (1982) as the record that "always stood out" (LAmag, 2018; Boston Globe, 2019). Speer "showed me the music I should listen to in order to play bass" (XLR8R, 2018), and Relix reports she drew on Roots Radics bassist Errol "Flabba" Holt. Dub as a form is the thing to understand here: Jamaican engineers of the 1970s, King Tubby first and his assistant Scientist after him, remixed existing songs by removing vocals and instruments, which "tends to emphasise the bass guitar", then ran what was left through echo and reverb with parts dropping in and out (Wikipedia, Dub music). Lee says this is exactly why it worked as a textbook: the dub format isolates each part and makes it easy for a beginner to follow (LAmag). The trait carried forward is not a particular Jamaican bass line; it is a hierarchy in which bass and drums are the song and everything else is treatment. That hierarchy became Khruangbin's writing order. Early songs were made by Speer sending a drum loop, Lee writing a bass line over it, and Speer chopping the bass into a pattern and only then playing guitar over it (XLR8R; Relix). Speer: "It's all bass and drums, and I adapt guitar changes to the basslines" (Coconuts, 2018). Lee: "all pocket", "No chops" (Live For Live Music, 2019). Her self-taught technique is part of the sound rather than a limitation she hides: she plays with two left-hand fingers tied together, compares it to James Jamerson's one-finger playing and calls the result "fun and bouncy" (Premier Guitar, 2024). The dub debt is documented at every step, and the band closed the loop in 2019 by releasing *Hasta El Cielo*, a dub version of their second album with two mixes by Scientist himself (Boston Globe). Evidence: documented.

**The grooves are slow and spare because the drummer is a Houston church musician who thinks of the band as playing breaks.** Johnson and Speer played together for years in a Houston gospel church band before Khruangbin existed; the sources differ on whether they met there or at a jazz cafe in 2004, but agree that church was where they played together (Relix, 2018; XLR8R; Music Connection, 2022). Johnson: "My sense of rhythm and timing formed in the church playing gospel" (Austin Chronicle, 2020), and "real gospel music IS funky" (Music Connection). He describes Houston's church circuit as where "most of the good musicians" work and get paid, with the same players working the R&B scene (Music Connection). The trait he brings is restraint: Speer's direction to him was "Just keep it simple and keep it moving" (Premier Guitar, 2018); Johnson: "I've never been a busy drummer" (Coconuts), no fills or solos, "The music tells you what it needs" (Live For Live Music); his foundation is James Brown's drummers Clyde Stubblefield and Jabo Starks (Austin Chronicle, 2020). On top of that sits hip-hop's way of hearing drums. Speer: "Breaks. That's it. We're playing breaks" (Austin Chronicle, 2020). Johnson says "Maria También" nods to the Incredible Bongo Band's "Apache", the 1973 record whose drum break "has been called 'hip-hop's national anthem'" (Austin Chronicle, 2020; Wikipedia, Apache). Then there is tempo. The band says the slowness is Houston. Johnson: "Our playing, our vocabulary, the way that we are, everything is influenced by Houston", and all three are "influenced by DJ Screw", the Houston DJ who from about 1991 slowed records to a crawl on his Screwtapes; Johnson says he put "subtle winks to DJ Screw" on the first two albums. Speer: "I'm from Houston, we *have* to slow it down!" Lee: "It's like swimming through molasses and you want the song to swim with you" (all Austin Chronicle, 2020; DJ Screw per Wikipedia). Evidence: documented for gospel, breaks, Screw and tempo, in the members' own words; which specific "winks" are Screw's is not stated.

**The guitar is reverb-heavy because it is standing in for a singer and the band is only three people.** Speer's own reasons are practical before they are stylistic. "There's only three of us, so I'm using ambient effects like reverb and delay to fill it out", and the guitar takes the lead vocal's place: "I'm specifically trying to make it sing" (Premier Guitar, 2018). He does not use amp reverb on tour because he never knows what amp he will get abroad, so the main reverb is an Electro-Harmonix Holy Grail on its spring setting, and the studio doubles that with real springs: "we use springs a lot" (Premier Guitar, 2018; Tape Op, 2022/2024). The reference he reaches for is not surf but the sound of instruments with sympathetic strings: "a sitar kind of has a built-in reverb. So does a rabab from Afghanistan", which he associates with a mystical quality (Premier Guitar, 2018; Tape Op). The other half of the reverb is literally a building. Everything is tracked live in the Speer family barn in Burton, a late-1800s structure with dirt floors and corrugated tin siding; open the doors for a drier sound, close them for room (Premier Guitar, 2018; Live For Live Music). "You can definitely hear the barn in the records", says Lee (Austin Chronicle, 2020), and for mixing Speer clapped his hands in the barn, made an impulse response from it, and ran everything through it (Tape Op). Engineer Steve Christensen records in mono with dynamic mics (Tape Op). The rest of the tone is a 2001 Stratocaster with DiMarzio Cruiser pickups wired "wrong" so some positions are out of phase, flatwound strings, a wah left on in its trebly toe-down position, and a Fender Deluxe Reverb (Premier Guitar, 2018; Mixdown, 2019). Evidence: documented, in Speer's words. The obvious comparison to surf and to The Shadows is only partly documented: Speer wanted "Maria También" to be in the spirit of "Apache", the Shadows' 1960 Stratocaster-and-tape-echo instrumental (Relix; Wikipedia, Apache), and Lee notes that the 1960s Thai music they love was itself shaped by touring surf acts (Music Connection). Beyond that one song, "surf" is a word writers use about him, not one he uses about himself.

**The songs are instrumental because none of them wanted to sing, and because the music they listen to is music they cannot understand the words of.** Speer: "I didn't want to sing"; "I have sung in bands before, and I don't really like it"; and as a listener, "I didn't want to have music with words that would distract me" (Tape Op). Lee: "most of the time I like listening to music without words or in another language", because understanding lyrics pulls her out of daydreaming (Music Connection); being instrumental makes the music "more of a universal experience" (Austin Chronicle, 2020) and early on "it seemed very subversive that we were instrumental" (Guitar.com, 2025). Premier Guitar (2018) reports, in paraphrase, that Speer is drawn to melody and vocal intensity in languages he does not understand. That listening preference is why the Thai and Iranian records matter, and it is also, on this reading, why the band could leave the vocal slot empty: the records they model themselves on are ones where the voice was already functioning as pure melody for them. That last link is interpretive; no member states it in those terms. What they do state is that vocals were resisted under label pressure and "added only where we saw fit" (XLR8R), and that the foundation remains an instrumental trio even with Leon Bridges on the EPs.

**Where Thailand actually fits.** The band started, by Lee's account, as "a total Thai obsession" (XLR8R): Speer found 1960s–70s Thai cassettes through Peter Doolan's blog Monrakplengthai, which has posted luk thung, molam and regional Thai styles recorded from tape since 2008, and he and Lee made mix CDs of it (Premier Guitar, 2018; Relix; the blog itself). The music they were hearing is itself a hybrid: luk thung is Thai country music that had taken on Western instruments, molam is the Lao/Isan form built on the khaen mouth organ and the phin lute, and in the 1970s molam in particular absorbed rock, soul and funk from American GIs stationed at bases in Isan, producing "trippy riffs, hypnotic patterns, and drawn-out, inventive solos" over mixes of phin, khaen, guitar, keyboard, bass and drums (Vice, 2016; Wikipedia, Luk thung and Mor lam; Record Collector, 2011). What Speer says he took from it is harmonic, not timbral: "in luuk thung and molam the changes don't line up as you'd expect", a style "challenging yet familiar at the same time", and he is explicit that he was never "pressing rewind to learn licks and phrases" but after "an overall vibe" (Coconuts, 2018). Lee says she learned bass while listening heavily to Thai music but does not think she plays "in a Thai way" except perhaps subconsciously (Coconuts). So the documented Thai inheritance is the feel of chord changes that arrive where a Western ear does not expect them, plus the example of a regional music that borrows Western instruments without becoming Western; the idea that Speer's tone copies Thai guitarists is not something any source supports. The second album moved the same method to Iran: Kourosh Yaghmaei's 1973 "Gole Yakh" "was a big influence on the songs of the record", and Speer hears in 1970s Iranian pop "that phaser guitar", "the Shaft hi-hat", and arrangements where drums are present but not heavy, while Lee hears "toughness of the bass and drums" under "romantic melancholy vocals" (Vinyl Factory, 2018). On this pass's reading, that is close to a description of Khruangbin's own recipe, which suggests they listen for records built the way they already build; no member puts it that way.

**What remains distinctive.** The combination is theirs, and two things in particular do not trace to any source studied here: the deliberate "space in the middle" of a three-piece (Johnson: the vibe comes from "the space within the music itself"; Speer: "There's space for mystery in there"), and Speer's habit of tracking a live trio in a barn and then "cut[ting] everything up in the computer" (Coconuts). Those are documented practices with no stated model.

**What you can now recognize.** When a Khruangbin song starts, listen for the order of events: drums and bass establish the song, and the guitar fits its chord changes to the bass line rather than the reverse. That order is documented; reading it as the dub hierarchy is this pass's link, resting on Lee's account of learning from dub's isolated parts. Her two-fingered, melodic lines ("loping, behind-the-beat" in Relix's words) were learned on Scientist and Barrington Levy records rather than from a bass method. Notice that the drummer never fills; Johnson says that restraint came from church and Speer calls what they play "breaks"; Relix hears loop-like precision in it. The tempo is Houston's by the band's own admission. Hear the reverb as two layers, a spring (Holy Grail live, real springs in the studio) and a tin-sided barn, and hear it as Speer's substitute for a singer's sustain, modelled on sitar and rubab rather than surf. When a chord change lands somewhere you did not expect, that is the trait Speer names as the one he took from luk thung and molam. And on "Maria También" specifically you can listen for "Apache" twice over, the Shadows' guitar idea and the Bongo Band's break, because that is the one song where the band has said so. Whether any given track does these things is for your ears; this pass did not listen.

**Sources studied, in their own right (where not covered above):**
- Scientist (Hopeton Brown, b. 1960): King Tubby's assistant from the mid-1970s, named by Bunny Lee ("this little boy must be a scientist"), moved to Channel One at the end of the decade; his early-80s dub albums, including *Scientist Wins the World Cup* (1982), were played by the Roots Radics, the band formed in 1978 by bassist Errol "Flabba" Holt that "dominated the sound in the first half of the 1980s" and cut Barrington Levy's first riddims in 1979 (Wikipedia, Scientist and Roots Radics). Neither article describes Holt's bass playing, so the Lee–Holt link rests on Relix's report alone.
- Kourosh Yaghmaei (b. 1946): self-taught guitarist, "Godfather of Iranian psychedelic rock", started in 1964 with a band covering The Ventures and The Kinks; "Gol-e Yakh" (1973) blends Persian melody with Western harmony, with the Vox Continental organ rather than guitar as rhythmic foundation; recorded 24 songs in 1975–79, reissued by Now-Again as *Back from the Brink* (2011) (Wikipedia). Googoosh (b. 1950): Iran's pre-revolution pop icon, banned from performing after 1979 until 2000 (Wikipedia); Speer on her: "her whole delivery is heartbreaking" (Vinyl Factory).
- DJ Screw (Robert Earl Davis Jr., 1971–2000): Houston DJ whose slowed mixes began in 1990, sold as Screwtapes from his house, tied to the city's codeine culture; Johnson links the slowness to that culture making things "sound better when they're slow and kaleidoscope-y" (Wikipedia; Austin Chronicle, 2020).
- Yppah (Joe Corrales Jr.): Ninja Tune electronic/rock producer known for "heavy use of reverb, effects, and electronics"; Speer brought Lee on his Yppah tour supporting Bonobo, after which she quit her job and asked "Can we start a band?" (Wikipedia; XLR8R). What playing in that band taught either of them is not stated anywhere read.
