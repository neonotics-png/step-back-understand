# The Wire, understood through what made it

Sources checked 2026-11-03. I have not watched The Wire. Everything below about what happens on screen comes from named written sources: interviews with David Simon and Ed Burns, episode and season summaries, and criticism.

**Subject and question.** The Wire (HBO, 2002–2008, five seasons, 60 episodes). Open question: why is it built the way it is (one case stretched across a season, a new part of the city added each year), why does it treat institutions as the real protagonists, and what makes critics call it different from other TV dramas? Each shaping source below is studied on its own first and then traced into the show.

---

## The explanation

### 1. A reporter's year inside one unit: *Homicide: A Year on the Killing Streets* (1991)

**The source itself.** In 1988 Simon, a Baltimore Sun police reporter, spent a year with one shift of the Baltimore Police homicide unit. A detective had told him that if someone "just wrote down what happens in this place for one year, they'd have a goddamn book." Simon said the aim was "the demythification of the American detective." The book follows real cases across a full year, including one unsolved child murder Simon called "the spine of the book." It also covers the politics inside the department. One feature matters a lot here: a whiteboard listing each detective's cases, open ones in red and closed ones in black, which supervisors used to measure productivity. Simon said the project taught him patience in research.

**What carried into The Wire.** Three things, all documented:
- **The form.** A year with one unit, one long case as the spine, and a cast of working professionals. That is very close to the season shape of The Wire, where one investigation runs a whole season. The structural parallel is my comparison (interpretive). The material borrowing is documented: the red/black board, the slang, and characters based on real detectives in the book (Jay Landsman under his own name; Bunk Moreland, based on Rick Requer).
- **The institution's scorecard.** In the book, the board is how the department sees its detectives. The Wire pushes this much further. Statistics become the language every institution uses to judge itself, whether or not the numbers mean anything. In Season 4, Prez, a cop turned teacher, sees that test prep in his school is the same thing as "juking the stats" at the police department.
- **Demythifying the detective.** The book's stated goal became the show's pitch. Simon sold it to HBO as "the anti–cop show," a reaction against the "usual good guys chasing bad guys framework."

### 2. What Simon was reacting against: network TV, including his own

**The source itself.** The book became NBC's *Homicide: Life on the Street* (1993–99), and Simon later wrote and produced for it. He called it a "remarkable drama," but said it did not reflect the book. By his account, network pressure meant bending the story to please the audience. NBC executives kept asking the writers, "Where are the victories?" (Guardian oral history, reprinted in the Irish Times, 2018). HBO, by contrast, depended on subscribers, not ratings, and Simon already had a working relationship with HBO from *The Corner*.

**What carried into The Wire.** The network's question became the show's rule. The Wire "avoided victories, preferring to show corruption, failure and decay" (Irish Times/Guardian). Simon described the usual American TV story as one where a rebel rises against the odds and is proved right: "That's the story we want to be told over and over again" (Vice, 2009). Simon also said the first job was to "teach folks to watch television in a different way." The Wikipedia series article lists the techniques: key events happen off-camera, there is no voice-over exposition, there are only two flashbacks, episodes rarely end on cliffhangers, and the music is mostly diegetic (heard inside the scene) rather than a score. Simon's view was that most TV is "a condescending medium" and that viewers like "being trusted to acquire information on [their] terms" (The Believer, 2007). Evidence: documented.

### 3. The cop and the teacher: Ed Burns

**The source itself.** Burns spent about twenty years as a Baltimore homicide and narcotics detective, after Army service in Vietnam. His expertise was long investigations of violent drug organizations using surveillance. He led the 1980s case against the drug lord Melvin Williams, whose arrest Simon covered for the Sun (Irish Times/Guardian; A.V. Club). Burns then taught seventh and eighth grade in Baltimore's public schools. He called that "probably the hardest thing that I've ever done." Colleagues there, he said, believed "You can't do anything for these kids." His own rule was "If you blame the kids, you should find another job" (The Ringer, 2017).

**What carried into The Wire.** The show's two ends both come from his life:
- **The wiretap.** Season 1 grows out of the Williams case, the case that first brought Simon and Burns together (composition/documented). The A.V. Club calls Williams the inspiration for drug kingpin Avon Barksdale. Williams later appeared on the show as the Deacon. Burns's frustration with police bureaucracy matched Simon's own as a police reporter (Wikipedia, citing the creators).
- **The school.** HBO's press material says Season 4 is "based in large part" on Burns's years teaching. The episode where Prez talks about "juking the stats" is credited to a story by Burns and Kia Corthron. The change: Burns's argument that poverty is the problem nobody will look at (Ringer) becomes a season where the school turns out to be one more institution measuring itself by numbers.

### 4. Greek tragedy, with institutions in place of the gods

**The source itself.** Athenian tragedy was civic theater. It was staged at the Great Dionysia, a state festival paid for by wealthy citizens, around an altar, as "a collective ritual of the polis." The chorus was drawn from the citizen body. Plays used myth to address current events. Hubris brings nemesis, a divine punishment. The three tragedians differ. Aeschylus kept a strict moral order, with Zeus usually the ethical force. In Sophocles, the hero's suffering is often left unexplained. Euripides experimented with form and gave his heroes troubled inner lives. Aristotle said tragedy works through pity and fear toward catharsis (all from the Wikipedia overview of Greek tragedy, which is a secondary source).

**What carried into The Wire.** This is the clearest influence the maker himself names, and he names it often:
- "The Wire is a Greek tragedy in which the postmodern institutions are the Olympian forces": the police department, the drug trade, city hall, the schools. These institutions "always prove larger" than the people who try to beat them (Simon to Nick Hornby, The Believer, August 2007).
- He called the institutions "the indifferent gods" and said the writers had "ripped off the Greeks" and applied tragedy to "the modern city-state" (New Yorker profile, Oct. 22, 2007, quoted via 3 Quarks Daily). He said he reread Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides before Season 1: "We were writing a Greek tragedy" (Vice, 2009). The September 2000 pitch to HBO already ended on "something that Euripides or O'Neill might recognize: an America, at every level at war with itself" (excerpt quoted by Kottke).
- He set this against the other prestige HBO dramas: "Our model is not quite so Shakespearean." In his account, *The Sopranos* and *Deadwood* focus on a central character's inner life, while the Greeks show people facing a rigged game (Believer).
- On catharsis: "The ancients valued tragedy, not merely for what it told them about the world but for what it told them about themselves." He argued that American drama instead tends to "exult the individual over his fate" (Slate, Brett Martin, 2013).

**How it changed.** The gods become impersonal systems: budgets, chains of command, stats, markets. Hubris becomes trying to reform the system, and nemesis comes as a transfer or a cut program, not a thunderbolt. The civic-chorus idea is my interpretation, not Simon's words. Adding a whole new institution each season widens the polis on stage, much as the Athenian audience watched its own city's questions acted out. Simon's closest statement of this is that the show tells the viewer "This is what you've built. Take a look at it" (Vice). One limit: in what I read, Simon names the three playwrights but no particular play. The documented influence is a stance (indifferent forces, doomed challengers), not a play-by-play lineage.

### 5. The newspaper that didn't sound the alarm: Simon's years at the Sun

**The source itself.** Simon was at the Sun from 1982 to 1995. He was a union captain in a 1987 strike and left bitter: "some sons of bitches bought my newspaper and it stopped being fun." He blamed editors John Carroll and Bill Marimow for damaging the paper (Wikipedia, citing Baltimore City Paper, 2003). He recalls Carroll asking for stories about "the Dickensian lives of children," which Simon called "pandering" (Vice).

**What carried into The Wire.** Season 5 makes the press the last institution examined: "the people who are supposed to be monitoring all this and sounding the alarm — the journalists" (New Yorker, via Wikipedia). The theme is "what stories get told and what don't and why it is that things stay the same" (Slate 2006, via Wikipedia). The newsroom includes a fabricating reporter, and an editor character is named after Simon's real former Sun editor Rebecca Corbett. Evidence: documented for the theme and for the Corbett connection. I did not verify that specific fictional editors are modeled on Carroll or Marimow, and the Sun itself denied the portrait. Critics called Season 5 the most didactic season (La Berge, 2010). David Zurawik of the Sun said the newsroom story lacked the show's usual "sociological insight."

### 6. The writers: journalists and crime novelists, not TV writers

Simon says The Wire "pursues the form of the modern, multi-POV novel." He says that is partly because its makers were "not by training or inclination television writers" (Believer). The room included novelists Richard Price, George Pelecanos and Dennis Lehane, and former Sun reporters Bill Zorzi and Rafael Alvarez. Reviewers had compared Price's *Clockers* to the show before he joined. Simon rejects the common "Dickensian" label, and the Carroll quote above suggests why: to him, "Dickensian" meant newspaper sentiment. Evidence: documented for the novel claim; I did not study the novelists' books, so how much each contributed is unresolved.

## What this reveals, and what is The Wire's own

Put the sources together and the show's design looks less like a style choice and more like an argument. The Homicide book supplied the unit of storytelling (a year, one shift, one spine case, a board of numbers). Burns supplied the two institutions he had lived inside, the wiretap squad and the classroom. The fight with NBC supplied the rule against victories. The Sun supplied the final institution and its grudge. Greek tragedy supplied the frame that ties these together: the protagonist is not a person but the city's machinery, and people who challenge it are destroyed.

What looks like The Wire's own, rather than inherited:
- **Expanding one city across seasons**, so every new institution adds to what came before instead of replacing it. Simon said that without Season 2's port, "we truly are doing just a cop show." William Julius Wilson, who taught the show at Harvard, valued it for showing how "gangs, police, unions, schools, and government" connect (Harvard Gazette, 2010, paraphrase).
- **Treating the drug crew as an institution too.** Michael Wood (London Review of Books, 2010) singles out Simon's "bureaucracy among the gangsters." Characters who survive are those who know "the system can't be beaten, only lived with." This is a critic's reading (interpretive).
- **The pairing of documentary method with tragic fatalism.** Neither the reporting nor the Greeks alone explains this. Wood offers a caution about the realism: "it isn't good because it's authentic, it looks authentic because it's good."

Why critics treat it as different is partly this argument and partly reception history. It drew modest ratings and won no major awards when it aired, and HBO spent its promotion on *The Sopranos*. It found its audience on DVD. In 2021 it topped BBC Culture's poll of 206 critics from 43 countries as the best TV series of the 21st century. Not everyone accepts Simon's framing: Wood calls "it's not a cop show" a rhetorical pitch, since the cops are "the heart of the thing."

## What you can now recognize

- **The stats.** When a commander, mayor or principal is judged by a number, you are watching the Homicide book's red-and-black board. In The Wire the numbers have become the institution's own measure of itself, and the show treats chasing them ("juking the stats") as the thing that makes reform impossible.
- **The missing victory.** When a reformer is punished, it is the show answering NBC's "Where are the victories?" on purpose. In Simon's frame it is nemesis for hubris, with the institution in the role of the god. Watch for whether the punishment comes from a villain or from a budget, a transfer or a cut program. The sources say it is usually the second.
- **What the structure is doing.** The slow, unexplained, off-camera storytelling is Simon's stated refusal to condescend, and the season-long case is the Homicide book's year-long shape. The new institution each year widens the cast and the tragedy.
- **What the "Dickens" and "Shakespeare" labels miss.** Simon rejects both on purpose. "Dickensian" meant newspaper sentiment to him, and "Shakespearean" meant a focus on one great character's inner life. What he claims instead is Greek tragedy, gods turned into institutions. Whether a given storyline really works that way is for you to judge; this pass did not watch any.
