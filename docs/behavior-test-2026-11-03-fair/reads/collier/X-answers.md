# Jacob Collier quiz: answers

1. A piano has only the fixed 12 notes, and "G half-sharp" falls between G and G-sharp, so a piano has no key there to land on. Collier gets there with a chain of chords (Hein's "four magical chords"). Each chord shares one tone exactly with the chord before it, and that shared tone is tuned purely, not to the piano. Each new chord is tuned around that shared note, so it comes out slightly sharper than the last one but still sounds in tune. The small upward drifts add up until the piece has moved from E to G half-sharp. Hughes calls this "contextual tuning".

2. Negative harmony treats major and minor as mirror images. You reflect the key across an axis between the tonic and the dominant (C and G in C major). That turns C major into C minor and turns G7 into a chord built on F minor (the minor iv), so a perfect V-I cadence becomes a plagal iv-I "Amen" cadence. The mirror idea comes from Ernst Levy's *A Theory of Harmony* (1985), and saxophonist Steve Coleman later coined the term. Coleman says he passed it to Barak Schmool, who taught Collier, but only Coleman's account supports that. Collier talks about a "polarity" between the overtone and undertone series and loves the "minor plagal cadence resolving". By Wikipedia's account, though, he doesn't apply the exact reflection method. He uses it as a way of thinking and a darker, plagal route home, not as a recipe.

3. (Partly not covered.) The explainer teaches only that moving one way around the circle of fifths makes music "feel brighter" and moving the other way brings in "the darkness of a key center". It says his "super-ultra-hyper-mega-meta Lydian" keeps stepping around the circle in four-note chunks, and that this idea is described only in secondary blogs. It never explains why Lydian counts as the brightest mode. My reasoning from what it does say: Lydian would be the mode pushed furthest in the bright direction around the circle. For arranging, the scale lets him treat brightness as relative to where you arrive from. Arriving in F major from D-flat feels like "the sun's come out", and arriving from A major feels like "the sun's gone in". So he can choose the route to a chord or key change for the emotional effect he wants.

4. It means any melody note can sit over any bass note if you choose the right chord around them. Collier shows this by walking one melody note, F, through about a dozen different chords. So in reharmonisation the melody stays fixed, and the question becomes which colour or degree of tension he wants under it, not which chord is "correct". Searching for a chord that already contains the melody note gives a short list of safe answers. His approach opens every bass note and harmony as a choice, including dense "clustery" chords where the melody note "fights" the cluster. (The second half is my reasoning from his statement and the F demonstration.)

5. He builds a dense chord first, then stacks it. He described "one chord with eight different notes in it, and then each note is sung 10 times", giving "walls of sounds... 80 tracks high" that blur into something like "a church organ". The habit starts with the school-choir story: he rebuilt "Blow the Wind Southerly" at home and sang every part himself, pitch-shifting the bass down. Behind that sit the family's four-part Bach chorales and Take 6's all-voice jazz chords, which he multiplied from six singers to one overdubbed voice. He tunes voices by hand in cents instead of using Auto-Tune, and he keeps tracks unbounced so he has more panning options. The tuning reason ties to his pure, contextual tuning: chords lock in by sharing purely tuned tones, and pitch can drift microtonally, as in the slide to G half-sharp. A grid set to the piano's 12 notes would undo that. In #IHarmU he followed singers between semitones instead of correcting them. Why he times the parts by hand is not covered.

6. The harmonizer was built by MIT Media Lab student Ben Bloomberg from a modified TC-Helicon board and three instances of Antares Harmony Engine. Collier sings one note and plays up to twelve keys, and his voice comes out on every note, "like I'm a spontaneous choir". He built it to recreate his studio layering in real time. That means he has to sing and pick out the voicing on the keys at the same moment. The audience choir came from wanting a bandmate after three years of touring alone. He gets the crowd to "sing chords and clap and... stamp and call and response", so he has to direct and arrange a large group of amateurs on the spot. A WBUR critic notes McFerrin did this before him, but Collier doesn't name McFerrin as the source. (What each demands of him in real time is my reasoning from these descriptions.)

7. The organising principle was the course of one day, not genre. Vol. 1 runs from before dawn to late morning with orchestra, Vol. 2 from afternoon to evening, Vol. 3 is "the dead of night" with electronics, and Vol. 4 is "the rebirth of the dawn". His stated challenge was making maximalism serve "a sense of narrative": "sometimes it's about prioritizing... one specific feeling" instead of using everything at once. On the production side, he builds from the ground up in Logic and hits its 253-audio-track limit, so he splits songs into separate projects per section. (The explainer doesn't say outright that this was the main challenge he saw. That reading is mine.)

8. Ethan Hein finds the harmonies "intellectually fascinating" but says Collier "pays too much attention to the chords and not enough to everything else". (Not covered: the explainer gives no Hein comparison with Wonder's original "Don't You Worry 'Bout a Thing". It says only that Collier's 2013 breakthrough cover was "pretty extremely reharmonised".) Reasoning from that: the trade-off is that the same thing that makes his work distinctive, the harmony rewritten under a fixed tune and pushed to extremes, can crowd out groove, melody and the song's own feel. The explainer leaves open whether his harmony serves the songs.
