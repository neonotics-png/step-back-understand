# Jacob Collier, understood through what shaped him

Sources checked 2026-11-03. I have not listened to or watched any recording or video. Every claim about how something sounds comes from a named written source: Collier's own words in interview transcripts, or critics' and researchers' descriptions. Where I draw a comparison myself, I say so.

**Subject and question.** Jacob Collier's music, in three parts: his harmony, his stacked vocals, and how he arranges songs. The question: where does each of these come from, and what can you listen for once you know?

## The explanation

### 1. Three childhood sources: a choir, a family that sang Bach, and Britten

Most of Collier's method shows up in one story he told NPR's TED Radio Hour (transcript, 2022-06-17). In his school choir he was "very irreverent": he would "write notes in the score that weren't in the music" and hand the parts back out, so the choir sang his "funky notes". The choir was given an arrangement of the British folk song "Blow the Wind Southerly". He kept thinking "you could add a G there. Or you could add an E there." He went home and rebuilt the arrangement from memory with all the notes he had imagined. His boy's voice couldn't reach the bass part, so he sang it an octave up and used a pitch-shifter plugin to bring it down.

That one afternoon has all three of his habits in it:
- he takes an existing song and harmonises it again (arranging as rebuilding);
- he sings every part himself (stacking);
- he adds notes the original didn't have (harmony as added colour).

He still describes his live work in those terms: "my first love of arranging and rebuilding" (Time Out Israel, 2017-05-08).

Two pieces of classical background sit under this:

- **Bach chorales.** Collier says, "We sing Bach chorales together as family" (quoted on Wikipedia's Collier page). A Bach chorale is a four-part, soprano–alto–tenor–bass setting of an existing hymn tune, with the tune on top and the other three voices supplying the harmony (Wikipedia, list of Bach's chorale harmonisations). His mother is a violinist and a professor at the Royal Academy of Music's Junior Academy, and she brought him up on her own. He says her approach to learning "really came from play rather than practice" (TED Radio Hour).
- **English choral music and Britten.** Collier says, "I was very inspired by English choral music as a boy, and also by the a cappella group Take 6 as a teenager" (Meer, 2019-09-04). As a boy treble he sang Miles in Benjamin Britten's opera *The Turn of the Screw*. Wikipedia quotes his reaction to Britten's harmony as "My mind was shattered outwards". I could not find the original interview behind that quote. Britten's opera is built on a twelve-note theme, and each scene opens with a variation on it. Its music "mixes tonality and atonality" (Wikipedia, *The Turn of the Screw*).

What that background means, on my reading (interpretive, not something Collier has said): the chorale gave him the basic move behind his arrangements, which is to keep the tune and rewrite everything underneath it. "In the Bleak Midwinter" (2016) is a strophic carol, meaning every verse is sung to the same tune. Ethan Hein's analysis (2019) puts the big change between the third and fourth verses. So that arrangement is the chorale move pushed much further: the same melody, with a different harmony under each verse. No source I found connects the Britten opera's theme-and-variations form to Collier's arranging. That parallel is unproven.

### 2. Take 6: where he says his harmony came from

**What Take 6 is, in its own right.** Take 6 is a six-man a cappella gospel group. It formed in 1980 at Oakwood College in Huntsville, Alabama, and released its first album in 1988. That album won Grammys in both the gospel and the jazz categories (Wikipedia, Take 6).

- **Who arranges.** Mark Kibble arranges most of the songs, and Mervyn Warren did too in the early years. Founder Claude McKnight says Kibble is "a genius at the sound of Take 6" (Chicago Symphony Orchestra interview, 2024-04-15).
- **Their influences.** McKnight names doo-wop, the Mills Brothers, the Golden Gate Jubilee Quartet, Manhattan Transfer, and Earth, Wind & Fire (CSO 2024). A 1989 Christian Science Monitor profile names Singers Unlimited, the Hi-Lo's, and Manhattan Transfer as the members' favourites. It describes the sound as "six Stevie Wonders performing with the panache of the Hi-Los or the Four Freshman".
- **How the sound works.** With no instruments, the voices supply everything. Alvin Chea sings the bass line, and the other voices add vocal percussion and imitate horns (Wikipedia). Their method is to train the ear "to hear those harmonies with a good working knowledge of keyboards and intervals" (McKnight, CSO 2024). Warren told the Monitor in 1989, "We are not relying on any accompaniment."

**One step further back: Singers Unlimited.** Take 6 members have credited this group as a cappella innovators (Wikipedia, The Singers Unlimited). Four singers, arranged by Gene Puerling, used overdubbing to "double/triple up" their voices into dense, uniform chords (Wikipedia, Gene Puerling). In other words, by the 1970s, recording a few voices over and over to build a big chord was already an established a cappella sound.

**Collier and Take 6.**
- He says, "Take 6 taught me a sense of harmony when I was a teenager. They were in some ways my greatest heroes" (ERR, 2024-07-20, said while touring with them).
- He also performed with them in 2017, on a "You and I" TV appearance (Wikipedia).
- How strong this link is: Collier gives a documented credit for harmony in general. But he hasn't named a specific Take 6 technique he took, so any particular trait below is my inference.

**What carried forward, and how it changed (interpretive).** The comparison suggests he took Take 6's core idea: jazz-dense chords built entirely from voices, with the voices also covering bass, rhythm, and horn parts. He changed two things:
- **One singer instead of six.** Take 6 is six people. Collier is one person, overdubbed. That is closer to Singers Unlimited's method than to Take 6's, but no source says Collier studied Singers Unlimited.
- **Far more layers.** He describes his stacks to NPR (All Things Considered, 2018-12-12) as "one chord with eight different notes in it, and then each note is sung 10 times", which makes "walls of sounds... 80 tracks high". That song, "Ocean Wide, Canyon Deep", stacks Laura Mvula's voice, not his. On "Moon River", the TED Radio Hour host said he layered his voice "like, 5,000 times". Collier says that layering turns the voice into "a new instrument... It sounds like a church organ."

### 3. Stevie Wonder and Bobby McFerrin

**Stevie Wonder.**
- Collier says "Stevie is my number one" (Time Out Israel, 2017).
- His breakthrough video in 2013 was a split-screen cover of Wonder's "Don't You Worry 'Bout a Thing". He describes it as "pretty extremely reharmonised" (Sound On Sound, Nov 2019).
- Wonder's own early-1970s albums were largely one-man studio productions. On *Music of My Mind* he "played all of the instruments himself", apart from a trombone and one guitar part (Wikipedia).
- The parallel with Collier recording every part alone in his bedroom is striking. But it is my comparison, not something either man has said.

**Bobby McFerrin.**
- Collier says he was "listening to a ton of Stevie Wonder and Bobby McFerrin at the time, so the two came together in my head". He said it while discussing how he learned to sing (Time Out Israel, 2017). That is a documented influence on his singing.
- McFerrin's signature traits: he sings the melody and the accompanying parts at once by jumping between registers, and he uses body percussion. *The Voice* (1984) was "the first solo vocal jazz album recorded with no accompaniment or overdubbing". "Don't Worry, Be Happy" uses "entirely overdubbed voice parts" and no instruments (Wikipedia pages for McFerrin and the song).
- Like Collier, McFerrin is a one-voice musician who builds whole textures from his own voice, live or overdubbed.

**The audience choir.** Collier's own account of where it came from is not McFerrin. He says that after three years touring alone, "all I wanted was a bandmate". He "started to jam with the audience", asking them "to sing chords and clap and... stamp and call and response" (TED Radio Hour, 2022). A WBUR critic notes that McFerrin "directed audience choirs long before Collier" (Chris Ritter, 2024-07-09). So McFerrin is a precedent a critic points to, not an influence Collier has stated.

### 4. Negative harmony, and Collier's own sense of brightness and darkness

**Where the theory comes from.** Ernst Levy's *A Theory of Harmony* (SUNY Press, 1985) treats major and minor as mirror images of each other. Saxophonist Steve Coleman later coined the term "negative harmony" for a related way of thinking. The mirror runs between the tonic and the dominant (C and G in C major). It turns C major into C minor, and it turns the dominant-seventh chord (G7) into something based on the minor fourth chord (F minor). So a "perfect" cadence (V to I, like G to C) turns into a "plagal" one (iv to I, like F minor to C), the "Amen" cadence (Wikipedia, Negative harmony).
- Wikipedia reports Coleman's claim that he passed the idea to educator Barak Schmool, "who taught Jacob Collier". This rests on Coleman's account alone; I found no confirmation from Collier or Schmool.

**What Collier does with it.**
- When asked about negative harmony in WIRED's "5 Levels" video (2018, read from an automatic transcript), he describes "polarity... between the undertone series and the overtone series". What he values in it is the feeling of a minor plagal cadence: "the feeling of a minor plagal cadence resolving is so moving". The transcript actually reads "the perfect in the playground" and "minor political cadence"; I take those to be mis-heard "perfect and the plagal" and "minor plagal".
- Wikipedia's Negative harmony article says Collier "has not explicitly applied the exact methods of negative harmony in his music". What he takes is the darker, plagal route home to the tonic.
- So Levy's theory shapes how Collier thinks and talks about harmony more than it supplies a mechanical recipe.

**His own frame: a brightness dial.** The more distinctive idea in the same WIRED transcript is his own. One direction around the circle of fifths makes us "feel brighter", and the other "is a lot more to do with the darkness of a key center". Arrival is relative: "if you arrive in F major from D-flat then it's like the sun's come out... but if you arrive in F major from A major then it's like the sun's gone in." He also says "every single melody note works with every single bass note", and he walks one melody note F through about a dozen different chords to show it. He gives his favourite sound to TED Radio Hour: dense, "clustery" chords where all the notes sit close together. He describes these as "tension and release at the same time" and says a sung note "fighting" the cluster is "such a beautiful fighting".

**Microtonality.** Collier says he has spent ten years "digging between those 12 notes", as "a deliberate effort to break my own perfect pitch open" (MusicTech, 2022-07-20). Ethan Hein's description of the "four magical chords" in "In the Bleak Midwinter" explains how it works:
- each chord is slightly sharper than the one before;
- each still sounds in tune, because it shares one tone exactly with the previous chord, tuned purely rather than to the piano;
- by the end, the piece has moved from E to "G half-sharp", halfway between G and G-sharp.

Madeleine Hughes's honours thesis (Edith Cowan University, 2020) analyses this piece, "Moon River" and "All I Need" as examples of "contextual tuning". The same idea appears in his crowd-sourced "#IHarmU" project, where he refused to pitch-correct the public's singing: "If they go between semitones, I go with them. I modulate microtonally to fit to their needs" (Sound On Sound, Nov 2019).

### 5. How he arranges

- **He thinks of the arrangement as a story.** The four *Djesse* albums follow the course of one day:
  - Vol. 1: before dawn to late morning, with orchestra;
  - Vol. 2: afternoon to evening;
  - Vol. 3: "the dead of night", with electronics;
  - Vol. 4: "the rebirth of the dawn".

  He says maximalism serves "a sense of narrative". "Sometimes it's about prioritizing... one specific feeling" (NPR, 2018-12-12). He also says, "voices is kind of where everything always begins". *Djesse Vol. 1* opens with a choir alone, "chord upon chord upon chord".
- **He builds the track from the bottom up, on a computer.** He started on Logic, a music-recording program, before he was a teenager, and he likes "building from the ground up". He keeps tracks unbounced, meaning he doesn't merge them down, "so there are more options for panning". He tunes voices by hand in cents, not with Auto-Tune (Sound On Sound, Nov 2019). He hits Logic's "253-audio track limit" and splits songs into a separate project per section (Mix, 2019-05-31).
- **A custom instrument lets him stack vocals live.** MIT Media Lab student Ben Bloomberg built him a vocal "harmonizer". Collier sings one note and plays up to twelve keys, and his voice comes out on every note: "like I'm a spontaneous choir" (TED Radio Hour; MIT Arts, 2016-12-06). Inside it is a modified TC-Helicon circuit board and three instances of Antares Harmony Engine software (Sound On Sound). He built it so he could "recreate the process of layering down these tracks... in real time" (TED Radio Hour). So his live sound is a translation of his studio method onto a stage.

### What is his own

The sources support this much. Each input was real:
- the choral and chorale habit of harmonising an existing tune, and the school-choir rebellion of adding notes;
- Take 6's all-voice jazz harmony;
- Wonder's and McFerrin's one-person approach;
- a theory vocabulary that came partly through negative harmony.

What Collier combined, by his own description and in work others have analysed, is the following:
- One singer stacking every note of very dense chords many times over, into "organ"-like walls.
- Harmony thought of as brightness and darkness relative to where you arrive from, rather than as a set of chord rules.
- Tuning treated as a variable he can move, as in the pure-tuned slide up to "G half-sharp".
- The audience treated as the next layer of the stack.

Not every listener values the result. Ethan Hein finds the harmonies "intellectually fascinating" but says Collier "pays too much attention to the chords and not enough to everything else" (2019). Whether Collier's harmony serves the songs is a question for your ears, not something this pass settles.

### What you can now recognise

- **Rebuilt covers.** When Collier covers a song, you can hear it as the "Blow the Wind Southerly" move. The tune stays roughly in place, the harmony under it is rewritten, and the same tune may get a different harmony each time it returns. That is a chorale habit taken to an extreme.
- **The stacks.** You can tell a Take 6-style texture (six separate men, with a dedicated vocal bass) from a Collier stack, which is one voice multiplied. NPR describes it as eight notes, each sung ten times, so the chord blurs into an organ-like mass. You can also link that blur to an older overdubbing tradition, Singers Unlimited, while knowing Collier has not named it.
- **Key changes.** You can listen for whether a key change feels like "the sun's come out" or "the sun's gone in", and ask which way around the circle of fifths he travelled to get there. That is his own stated way of thinking.
- **Plagal endings.** When a cadence lands through a minor iv chord (the "Amen" shape with a minor colour) instead of the usual V-to-I, you can connect it to the negative-harmony idea he describes. But keep in mind that sources say he doesn't apply the exact reflection method.
- **Drifting pitch.** In "In the Bleak Midwinter" and similar pieces, if the pitch seems to slide out of the piano's tuning while each chord still sounds in tune, that is the shared-pure-tone method Hein describes. Whether a given passage does this is for you to check by listening; I did not listen.
