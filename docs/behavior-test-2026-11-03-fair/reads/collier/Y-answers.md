# Jacob Collier quiz: answers

1. A normally tuned piano uses equal temperament, so its keys only sit on the 12 fixed semitones. A key a quarter tone between G and G♯ simply doesn't exist on it. Collier gets there with four chords. Each one keeps a note exactly in common with the chord before, but the chord is tuned purely (just intonation) around that note, so it lands slightly sharper than its equal-tempered version. Each step sounds in tune with the one before, but the small sharpenings add up until the pitch centre has moved a quarter tone, which is how "G half-sharp" major arrives between verses 3 and 4.

2. Negative harmony mirrors every note of a chord around an axis between the tonic and the fifth. In C that axis sits between E and E♭, so a G7 (dominant) becomes an Fm6-type chord (minor plagal), and the cadence's "pull" comes from the opposite side. The theory came from Ernst Levy and Steve Coleman, and Collier reportedly learned it through a teacher; his 2017 June Lee interview made it popular. He uses it as a way of thinking, a source of colourful alternative chords that still resolve, and not as a strict procedure for writing whole songs by mirroring.

3. On one root the seven modes run from brightest to darkest: Lydian, Ionian, Mixolydian, Dorian, Aeolian, Phrygian, Locrian. Each step darker lowers one note by a semitone and each step brighter raises one, and each step is one move around the circle of fifths. Lydian is brightest because of its raised 4th (#11). "Super-ultra-hyper-mega-meta Lydian" asks what happens if you keep sharpening notes further up the circle of fifths, beyond the 7-note system. In arranging, this gives him a dial for mood: moving up in fifths reads as "brighter" (a lift, e.g. at an emotional peak) and moving down reads as "darker" (heavy or tender moments, like borrowed minor iv or ♭VI).

4. (not covered) The explainer doesn't give this quote. From what it teaches about reharmonization, I'd read it as: any melody note can take a different role (root, 3rd, 5th, maj7, 9th, #11, 13th, ♭9) over some bass and chord, so every bass note is a possible choice under every melody note, each with its own emotional colour. That differs from "find the chord that contains the melody note" because you don't start from a short list of diatonic chords that fit. You start from the feeling you want and choose the bass and chord that gives the melody note the role producing it. With clusters, polychords and extensions, even tense pairings (like ♭9) become usable colours.

5. He records every part himself, one track at a time, building a choir from his own voice: from 4–8 close-harmony parts up to dozens of overdubs (and, on "Moon River", thousands of voices including fans'). He doesn't just stack block chords. He splits the parts into roles: a sustained pad, a bass voice, independently moving inner lines, a lead, vocal percussion, with breaths and consonants used as texture. The habit draws on Take 6 and gospel choirs (close harmony), jazz piano voicings (Bill Evans, Herbie Hancock), Bobby McFerrin (one voice as a band), and Brian Wilson / Queen (stacked studio vocals). He tunes by ear against the other parts so the chords lock into just intonation (e.g. pure major thirds lower than piano thirds), which gives the ringing, overtone-rich sound that equal-tempered grid correction would destroy. (Timing by hand versus quantizing isn't covered; the explainer only discusses tuning.)

6. The Harmonizer, built with Ben Bloomberg (MIT Media Lab, from about 2014), takes his live voice and turns it into chords of up to about 12 notes in real time, driven by what he plays on a keyboard. So he has to sing the line and play the harmony with his hands at the same moment, choosing the voicing live (Bloomberg also built the looping one-man-band rig). The audience choir means splitting the crowd into sections, giving each a note with hand signals, and then moving them through chord changes, sometimes into extended chords, in real time. That requires him to conduct and voice-lead thousands of untrained singers on the spot, treating each person as one held note in a chord.

7. The organizing principle was an arc from day into night across four volumes, each with its own sonic world: Vol. 1 orchestral and choral (Metropole Orkest, Take 6), Vol. 2 acoustic/folk ("Moon River"), Vol. 3 electronic/R&B/pop, Vol. 4 a mix of everything with audience choirs and many guests. He treated guests as orchestral colours within one large design, not as verse swaps. (not covered) The explainer doesn't say what he saw as the main challenge. My best inference is keeping one coherent emotional arc and identity across very different sound worlds and many collaborators.

8. (not covered) The explainer doesn't present any critique of Collier. It mentions Ethan Hein's comparison of "Don't You Worry 'Bout a Thing" only as noting Stevie's original voice-leading, where chords descend while the top notes stay put. My best reasoning from what it teaches: because he changes chords much more often than the original and packs in dense, distant harmony, a likely critique is that the reharmonization can overwhelm the song, trading Stevie's simple, economical voice-leading and groove for constant harmonic surprise. The trade-off is richness and novelty against clarity and emotional directness.
