# Understanding Jacob Collier: harmony, stacked vocals, and arrangement

I haven't listened to any of these recordings. What follows comes from published analysis, interviews, and Collier's own explanations, plus general music theory. Where I'm unsure of a detail, I say so. Use the "what to listen for" notes as a guide while you listen yourself.

---

## 0. The one idea that explains most of it

Collier treats **a melody as fixed and everything under it as negotiable.** Most of his famous moments follow from that. He takes a tune you already know (a standard, a Stevie Wonder song, a carol). He keeps the melody recognisable. Then he asks what *emotional context* each melody note could have, and changes that context from moment to moment. That context is the harmony, the voicing, the key and even the tuning.

He has a lot of technique, but it serves this one aim: **he uses harmony to steer emotion**, and treats it as more than decoration. He talks about chords as "brighter" or "darker", "warmer" or "colder", and much less in terms of function (tonic, dominant and so on). Once you hear his choices as moves along that bright/dark scale, they stop sounding random.

## 1. Harmony

### 1.1 One melody note, many meanings (reharmonization)

The core skill is **reharmonization**: putting a different chord under the same melody note. A single note, say C, can be:

| The C is the... | Example chord under it | Rough feeling |
|---|---|---|
| root | C major | home, plain |
| 3rd | A♭ major | warm, a bit wistful |
| 5th | F major | open, settled |
| major 7th | D♭maj7 | dreamy, suspended |
| 9th | B♭add9 / B♭maj9 | soft, lifted |
| #11 | G♭maj7#11 (Lydian) | very bright, floating |
| 13th | E♭13 | bluesy, rich |
| ♭9 | B7♭9 | tense, wants to move |

Collier's WIRED "5 Levels of Difficulty" video on harmony (2017) is built on this idea: he sings one note and keeps changing what sits under it. When people say his arrangements make a familiar tune "feel new", this is usually the mechanism. The melody is unchanged, but each note's *role* keeps shifting.

**What to listen for:** take any cover he does of a well-known song and follow only the melody. Notice that it hardly changes while the chords underneath change much more often than in the original. That's the "harmonic rhythm".

### 1.2 Dense, extended, close voicings

His chords tend to be:
- **Extended**: 9ths, 11ths, 13ths and altered tones (♭9, #9, #11, ♭13), as in jazz and gospel.
- **Clustered**: notes a 2nd apart, packed closely together. The rub between neighbouring notes gives the "shimmer" people describe.
- **Polychordal / upper-structure**: one triad sounding over a different bass or chord (for example D major over C, which gives C Lydian colour). This is the jazz-pianist habit of thinking "triad on top, shell below", applied to voices.
- **Gospel-inflected**: passing chords, chromatic approach chords and "walk-ups" like those in Take 6 and church choir writing. Collier has named Take 6 as a big influence, and they sing on his "All Night Long" arrangement.

A few useful lineages: **Take 6 and gospel choirs** (close vocal harmony), **Bill Evans / Herbie Hancock / Brad Mehldau** (jazz piano voicings), **Stevie Wonder** (chromatic soul harmony), **Bobby McFerrin** (one voice as a whole band), and **Brian Wilson / Queen** (stacked studio vocals).

### 1.3 Bright and dark: his Lydian ("super-ultra-hyper-mega-meta Lydian") idea

In interviews, especially June Lee's 2017 interview, which went viral, Collier lays out a way of thinking about **modes as a brightness scale**. The seven diatonic modes on one root, brightest to darkest:

**Lydian → Ionian (major) → Mixolydian → Dorian → Aeolian (minor) → Phrygian → Locrian**

Moving one step darker lowers one note by a semitone, and moving one step brighter raises one. Each step is one move around the circle of fifths. Lydian is the brightest diatonic mode because of its raised 4th (#11). His tongue-in-cheek "super-ultra-hyper-mega-meta Lydian" asks: *what if you keep going brighter past Lydian?* You keep sharpening notes by moving further up the circle of fifths, which takes you outside the normal 7-note system. I couldn't find a full written source for exactly how he defines each level, so take the details as a description of the idea and not his exact formula. The practical point is that **moving up in fifths reads to him as "brighter" and moving down in fifths as "darker"**, and he uses that to control mood.

**What to listen for:** moments where a chord suddenly feels like it "lifts" or "lights up" are often a move to Lydian or #11 colour. Moments that suddenly feel heavy or tender are often borrowed darker chords, such as minor iv or ♭VI in a major key.

### 1.4 Negative harmony

The same 2017 interview made **negative harmony** popular. The theory came from Ernst Levy and Steve Coleman, and Collier reportedly picked it up through a teacher. The idea is to mirror every note around an axis between the tonic and the fifth (in C, the axis sits between E and E♭). Mirrored this way, a G7 (dominant) becomes an Fm6-type chord (minor plagal), so the "pull" of a cadence comes from the opposite side. Collier has said he uses it more as a *way of thinking*, a source of colourful alternatives to standard progressions, than as a strict procedure. So don't expect whole songs written by mechanical mirroring. It's one tool for finding unusual chords that still resolve.

### 1.5 Modulation as a journey

Collier changes key **often, and to distant keys**, but he usually makes it feel smooth. Typical methods:
- **Common-tone pivots**: keep one or two notes fixed while the chord around them changes, so the ear has a thread to follow even into a remote key.
- **Chromatic voice-leading**: every voice moves by a semitone or a whole tone, so the "jump" is really many small steps.
- **Climactic lifts**: a big key change into the final chorus, often to a far-off key and not the cliché "up a semitone".

### 1.6 Microtonality and tuning (the "G half-sharp" moment)

The best-known example is his a cappella **"In the Bleak Midwinter"** (2016). Between the 3rd and 4th verses he modulates from **E major to "G half-sharp" major**, a key a *quarter tone* between G and G♯, which normal equal-tempered keys can't reach. As Ethan Hein's analysis describes it, he does it with **four chords**. Each one keeps a note *exactly in common* with the chord before but is tuned slightly sharper, using the difference between **just intonation** (pure, harmonic-series intervals) and **equal temperament**. Each chord sounds in tune with the one before, yet the whole pitch centre ends up a quarter tone higher. Collier explains it himself in a video Hein links.

The bigger point is that because he sings every part himself and tunes by ear, he can **tune chords purely**, as a barbershop or gospel group does. That's part of why his stacks sound so resonant. Pure major thirds sit noticeably lower than piano thirds, and the chord "rings" in a way equal temperament can't.

### 1.7 Rhythm (the half people forget)

The harmony gets the attention, but much of his arranging is **rhythmic**:
- Polyrhythms and cross-rhythms, like 3-against-4 grooves and layered hand percussion.
- **Metric modulation**, where the felt pulse shifts so the tempo seems to change while the underlying grid stays put.
- Odd groupings within a familiar groove.
- Using everyday objects and body percussion as the rhythm section, especially in the early YouTube and *In My Room* era.

## 2. Stacked vocals

### 2.1 What "stacked" means in his work

Collier records **every vocal part himself, one track at a time**, building a choir out of his own voice. That can be anything from 4–8 close-harmony parts to dozens of overdubs. The early split-screen YouTube videos (around 2011–2014, such as "Isn't She Lovely" and "Don't You Worry 'Bout a Thing") made the method visible: each box is one part.

Then **"Moon River"** (2019, *Djesse Vol. 2*) went much further. It combines thousands of voices, many sent in by fans and other musicians, layered into one huge arrangement. It won a Grammy for arranging.

### 2.2 Why the stacks sound the way they do

1. **One voice, one timbre, tight blend.** All the parts come from the same throat, so vowels, vibrato and tone match far better than in a real choir. The result can sound almost synthetic, like an "organ of voices".
2. **Pure tuning** (see 1.6). Tuning by ear to the other parts lets the chords lock into just intonation, which gives the ringing, overtone-rich quality.
3. **Close voicings with clusters.** Parts packed within an octave, with 2nds rubbing, give the shimmering, "glowing" texture.
4. **Range tricks.** He has a very wide range, from deep chest voice up to falsetto. For the very lowest or highest parts, some producers in this style also use pitch-shifting or octave doubling. I can't confirm which specific Collier tracks do this, so treat it as a possibility and not a fact.
5. **Contrasting vocal roles.** He doesn't just stack block chords. He splits the "choir" into **sections**: a pad of sustained "oohs" or "ahs", a **bass voice** doing the bass player's job, **inner lines** moving independently, a **lead**, and often **vocal percussion**. That's orchestration done with voices.
6. **Breath and texture as sound.** Breaths, consonants, whispers and percussive syllables are written into the texture as part of the music.

### 2.3 Live: the harmonizer

To perform this alone, Collier worked with **Ben Bloomberg**, then at the MIT Media Lab, from about 2014. Together they built a custom **vocal harmonizer** that takes his live voice and turns it into chords of up to ~12 notes in real time, driven by what he plays on a keyboard. Collier has described it as somewhere between a vocoder and a live sampler. Bloomberg also built the looping one-man-band show, and later a bigger live rig for his touring band.

### 2.4 The audience choir

In concert, Collier conducts the **audience as a choir**. He splits the crowd into sections, gives each a note with hand signals, and then moves them through chord changes, sometimes into rich extended chords, in real time. It's the stacked-vocal idea scaled up to thousands of untrained singers, and recorded audience choirs from his tours appear on *Djesse Vol. 4*. It also shows how he thinks: **a chord is a set of people each holding one note, and harmony means moving those people**.

## 3. How he arranges a song

### 3.1 Typical process (as he describes it and as it shows in his work)

1. **Start from the melody and the feeling.** Pick the emotional story first, say "this should start lonely and end in euphoria", then use harmony, key and texture to tell it.
2. **Reharmonize phrase by phrase.** Treat each melody note as a choice point (section 1.1). Plain chords where the lyric is simple, surprising ones where the lyric turns.
3. **Plan an arc of density.** Begin sparse (one voice, a piano, a ukulele) and add layers until the climax, often a full-texture "everything at once" moment. Then pull back for an intimate ending.
4. **Use key changes as structure.** The big lifts are placed where the song needs emotional lift, not simply at the last chorus.
5. **Orchestrate by role.** Bass, pad, inner movement, lead, rhythm. Each role can be a voice or an instrument he plays himself (piano, bass, drums, guitar, mandolin, percussion, synths). On *In My Room* (2016), he recorded almost everything alone in his bedroom in London.
6. **Leave space for surprise.** A sudden silence, a tempo or meter shift, a key change to somewhere unexpected, or a quote from another song.

### 3.2 The *Djesse* series as one large arrangement

*Djesse* (four volumes, 2018–2024) is designed as an arc from day into night, and each volume has a different sonic world:
- **Vol. 1**: orchestral and choral, with the **Metropole Orkest** (conducted by Jules Buckley) and guests such as **Take 6** ("All Night Long", a Grammy winner for arranging).
- **Vol. 2**: acoustic and folk-leaning, including "Moon River".
- **Vol. 3**: electronic, R&B and pop, including "He Won't Hold You" (with Rapsody, a Grammy winner) and "All I Need".
- **Vol. 4**: a mix of everything, with recorded audience choirs and many guests. Its "Bridge Over Troubled Water" (with John Legend and Tori Kelly) won the 2025 Grammy for Best Arrangement, Instrumental or A Cappella, and the album was nominated for Album of the Year.

He also won Grammys in 2017 for "Flintstones" and "You and I". Nearly all of his wins (seven by 2025) are in **arrangement** categories. That tells you where his main gift is: transforming material.

### 3.3 Collaboration as orchestration

On the *Djesse* albums he treats featured artists the way an orchestrator treats instruments. Each guest's voice or style becomes one colour in a larger design, and isn't a "verse swap". That's why the albums span gospel, folk, R&B, Indian classical (for example "A Rock Somewhere" with Anoushka Shankar and Varijashree Venugopal) and orchestral writing.

## 4. A listening plan

Each pick is followed by what to focus on. Listen once for enjoyment, then again with that focus.

1. **"Don't You Worry 'Bout a Thing"** (early YouTube split-screen). *Focus: reharmonization of Stevie Wonder's chromatic progression. Watch which box (part) moves when a chord changes.* Ethan Hein's analysis compares it with Stevie's original voice-leading, where the chords descend while the top notes stay put.
2. **"In the Bleak Midwinter"** (2016). *Focus: pure-tuned a cappella stacks, and the quarter-tone modulation between verses 3 and 4. Try to notice that the last verse sits "between" keys.*
3. ***In My Room*** (2016). *Focus: one person playing every role. Pick out the bass voice versus the pads versus the lead.*
4. **"All Night Long"** (*Djesse Vol. 1*, with Take 6 and the Metropole Orkest). *Focus: gospel close harmony against an orchestra.*
5. **"Moon River"** (*Djesse Vol. 2*). *Focus: a huge vocal texture serving a very simple melody, and how the density grows and shrinks.*
6. **"He Won't Hold You"** or **"All I Need"** (*Djesse Vol. 3*). *Focus: the same harmonic thinking moved into R&B and electronic production.*
7. **"Bridge Over Troubled Water"** (*Djesse Vol. 4*). *Focus: arranging a famous song for guest voices, plus his live-crowd-choir writing.*
8. Any **live audience-choir clip**. *Focus: watch him "voice" a crowd into an extended chord and then move it.*
9. **WIRED "5 Levels of Difficulty: Harmony"** and the **June Lee interview** (2017). Collier explains these ideas himself.

## 5. Hands-on exercises (the fastest way to really get it)

1. **One note, eight chords.** Sing or play a single sustained note (say C). Under it, play the chords from the table in 1.1 one after another. Name the feeling of each. This is the core Collier exercise.
2. **Reharmonize a nursery rhyme.** Take "Twinkle Twinkle" and change the chord on every other melody note, using common tones and stepwise bass motion. Aim for "surprising but smooth".
3. **Brightness ladder.** On one root, play the seven modes from Lydian down to Locrian. Feel each step get darker. Then try a progression that "brightens" (moves up in fifths) at an emotional peak.
4. **Four-part close-harmony stack.** Record yourself singing a I–vi–ii–V in four close parts, one track at a time. Then add a 9th to each chord as a fifth part. Hear how the 2nds create shimmer. Try tuning the major thirds slightly flat to hear them "lock".
5. **Negative-harmony swap.** Write a ii–V–I in C, then mirror the V (G7) around the E/E♭ axis to get the Fm6-type chord, and resolve that to C. Compare the two cadences.
6. **Density arc.** Arrange 16 bars of a song in three passes: (a) melody + bass only, (b) add pads, (c) add inner lines + percussion + a key change. That's his build-up shape in miniature.

---

## 6. Short summary

- **Harmony:** the melody stays put while each note gets new meaning through extended, clustered, gospel- and jazz-derived chords. He thinks in terms of brightness (modes and fifths), uses negative harmony as a source of ideas, makes distant modulations smooth with common tones, and occasionally moves pitch microtonally through just intonation.
- **Stacked vocals:** he overdubs every part himself, so the timbre matches and the tuning is pure. He splits his voice into orchestral roles (bass, pads, inner lines, lead, percussion). Live, he uses a custom MIT-built harmonizer and turns audiences into choirs.
- **Arrangement:** he starts from the emotional arc and uses harmony, key changes and density to tell it. He plays nearly every role himself and treats collaborators as orchestral colours. *Djesse* applies that approach across four whole albums.
