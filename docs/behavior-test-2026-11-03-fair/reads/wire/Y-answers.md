# The Wire: Quiz Answers (Y)

1. In Simon's version, the "gods" are the postmodern institutions: the police department, the drug trade, City Hall, the school system, the newspaper and the economy. As in Greek tragedy, a character's strength or virtue barely matters, so characters who challenge things (McNulty, Colvin, the classroom program) are punished, sidelined or absorbed rather than winning. Most TV dramas let the brave individual beat the system and deliver justice. *The Wire* shows decent, smart people losing to systems that have their own momentum.

2. The expanding structure builds a map of the whole city, so that by the end Baltimore itself is the protagonist and you can see how its institutions connect and fail together. Season 2's dockworkers show the economic root of the drug trade. With the factories and the port in decline ("We used to make shit in this country"), blue-collar work is disappearing, and that is why the corner becomes one of the few jobs left for young men. Frank Sobotka also turns to smuggling to save his union, which mirrors how people in a dying economy end up in crime. So the port season explains why the corners exist. It is not a detour.

3. "Juking the stats" means that once an institution is judged by numbers, people manage the numbers instead of the problem, for example police downgrading felonies so crime appears to fall. It returns in Season 4, when a former cop turned teacher sees schools "teach to the test," and the same pattern of chasing a measurable target over the real mission appears at the paper (prizes) and City Hall (elections). It shows that institutions fail mainly through incentives and self-preservation, not villainy. Ordinary people are rewarded for protecting the institution's image and their own careers, so the system produces bad results even without anyone being especially corrupt.

4. Both are hierarchies with chains of command, middle managers, numbers to hit and expendable people at the bottom. Concretely, Stringer Bell runs crew meetings using parliamentary procedure, and police commanders hold "stat" meetings that work like drug-crew accountability sessions; D'Angelo's chess lesson (the king stays king, pawns get taken early) applies equally to both sides. The parallel matters because the show's argument is that institutions, not individuals, are the problem. A good-vs-evil setup would suggest that catching the bad guys fixes things, while the mirror shows both sides are governed by the same logic and both grind down the people inside them.

5. (Partly not covered: the explainer does not quote Simon on what the two storylines share.) From what it teaches, both are attempts to reform how the drug game works from inside an institution: Colvin tries to change policing by quietly legalizing drugs, and Stringer tries to make the drug trade a rational, legitimate business. Both fail because the surrounding systems won't change their nature. Hamsterdam actually reduced violence in the rest of Colvin's district, but it was shut down and turned into a scandal because it embarrassed the department and City Hall, an example of reform getting absorbed and self-preservation coming before the mission.

6. It is not simply fatalistic, because the show is bleak about systems but keeps individual morality and choice in play. The montage shows the system reproducing itself with new people in old roles, yet the same finale also lets Bubbles get clean and be allowed upstairs to his sister's dinner table, and Namond escapes the corner when Colvin takes him in. Characters like Omar and Bunk live by codes the show respects, and Carver grows into a thoughtful cop, even though the system limits what that growth can do. Simon's own reply to the fatalism charge is that he is diagnosing a failed system, not saying people shouldn't try. (The specific "cynical about institutions" quote is not in the explainer, but this matches what it teaches.)

7. Leaving jargon unexplained drops the viewer into the city as it really is, which serves the show's reportorial realism and its sense that these worlds have their own rules that outsiders must learn. Using only source music and almost no voiceover or flashbacks denies the viewer emotional cues and easy catharsis, which fits the show's view that TV's neat payoffs are a lie about how cities work. Treating episodes as chapters lets consequences build slowly and pay off seasons later, which is how institutions actually work: slowly, through cause and effect across many people, with no case of the week resolved by a single hero. The form makes you see systems rather than individual episodes of justice.

8. The newspaper season asks why a city can't see its own problems, so the press is the final institution: the one that should hold all the others to account. The shrinking *Sun* misses the real story and chases prizes, the same self-preservation and stat-chasing seen in the police and schools, and the paper buys a detective's invented serial killer while ignoring the actual failures the show has spent four seasons depicting. It also connects to the "gutting of local newspapers" the show targets and to Simon's own background as a *Sun* reporter. The main criticism was that it was heavy-handed, partly because Simon seemed to be settling scores with his former editors, and that the fake serial-killer plot was implausible, making Season 5 the weakest for many viewers.
