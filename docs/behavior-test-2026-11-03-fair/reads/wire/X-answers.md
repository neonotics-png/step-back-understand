# The Wire quiz: answers

1. In Simon's version the "gods" are the institutions: the police department, the drug trade, city hall, the schools. He calls them "the indifferent gods," and they "always prove larger" than the people who try to beat them. So a character who challenges the system is not rewarded the way a rising rebel is in most American TV, which "exult[s] the individual over his fate." Trying to reform the system is the hubris, and the nemesis that follows is usually impersonal, such as a transfer, a budget cut or a cancelled program, not a villain's defeat.

2. The structure builds one connected city. Each new institution adds to the ones already shown instead of replacing them, so by the end the viewer sees how "gangs, police, unions, schools, and government" fit together (William Julius Wilson's reading), the whole "modern city-state" as a tragic polis. Simon said that without Season 2's port, "we truly are doing just a cop show." So the dockworkers widen the frame from cops and dealers to the wider city that produces them, which is the show's real subject. (The explainer gives no detail about the dockworkers' own storyline, so the specifics beyond Simon's quote are my reasoning.)

3. "Juking the stats" means manipulating the numbers an institution uses to judge itself so that they look good, whether or not anything real has improved. It goes back to the Homicide book's red/black clearance board, and in The Wire statistics become every institution's measure of itself. In Season 4, Prez, a cop turned teacher, sees that test prep at his school is the same thing as juking the stats at the police department. It shows that institutions fail because people are rewarded for hitting the numbers, not for doing the work. Chasing the stats is what makes reform impossible even when no one is especially corrupt.

4. The explainer says the show treats the drug crew as an institution too. Michael Wood points to Simon's "bureaucracy among the gangsters," with hierarchy, chains of command and rules, like the police department's. Both sides also reward the same survival skill: the people who last are the ones who know "the system can't be beaten, only lived with." This matters because Simon pitched an "anti-cop show" against the "good guys chasing bad guys framework." If both sides are institutions that crush individuals, the story is about systems rather than heroes and villains. (Partly not covered: the explainer gives no specific scene-by-scene parallels, so the two examples are drawn from Wood's general reading.)

5. (not covered) The explainer does not mention Hamsterdam, Colvin or Stringer Bell. From its framework, both storylines would be people trying to reform or rationalize an institution from inside, which is the hubris that brings nemesis. A program that works by its own measures can still be ended by the larger institution (politics, chain of command, a cut program), because in Simon's frame the institution, not the reformer, decides what counts.

6. (not covered for the quote and the finale montage) From what the explainer teaches, the show is fatalistic about systems: the institutions "always prove larger," and people who leave are replaced. But it is not fatalistic about people. Burns's rule, "If you blame the kids, you should find another job," and the Season 4 school story treat individuals as worth fighting for, and the show's purpose, telling viewers "This is what you've built. Take a look at it," assumes people can recognize their situation and respond. The tragedy works by caring about individuals while showing that the machine they live inside doesn't.

7. Simon said the first job was to "teach folks to watch television in a different way." He called most TV "a condescending medium" and said viewers like "being trusted to acquire information on [their] terms." Leaving slang and procedure unexplained, using no voice-over, putting key events off-camera, using diegetic music and avoiding cliffhangers all refuse to tell the viewer how to feel or where the victory is. That fits the argument that this is a documentary-style look at a city's machinery, not a story built to deliver a satisfying win. Chapter-like episodes also match the "multi-POV novel" form and the Homicide book's year-long single-case shape, where meaning builds slowly across a whole case and city.

8. The press is the last institution because it is "supposed to be monitoring all this and sounding the alarm," so ending with it asks why nobody told the story of everything shown in Seasons 1 to 4. The theme is "what stories get told and what don't and why it is that things stay the same." The newsroom, which has a fabricating reporter and editors chasing prizes and "Dickensian" sentiment instead of systemic stories, is one more institution that games its own measures. It comes from Simon's bitter years at the Sun. Critics called it the most didactic season, and the Sun's David Zurawik said it lacked the show's usual "sociological insight." The Sun also denied the portrait.
