# Jacob Collier Quiz: Answer Key

## 1. The quarter-tone modulation in "In the Bleak Midwinter"

**Model answer:** A piano is fixed in 12-tone equal temperament, so it has no key between G and G-sharp. Unaccompanied voices are not fixed. Collier uses the gap between equal-tempered intervals and pure intervals from the harmonic series (just intonation). Each chord in the passage is tuned pure, and each new chord shares one pitch exactly with the chord before it. Because every chord is tuned pure against the held pivot note, each one comes out slightly sharp of the previous one. A few such steps add up to about a quarter tone, so the music lands in "G half-sharp" without any step sounding out of tune.

**Key points for full credit:**
- Equal temperament (piano) has no quarter-tone keys. The move depends on free intonation and pure (just) intervals, not 12-TET.
- The mechanism is chord-to-chord: each chord shares an exact common tone with the previous one, and tuning each chord pure around that pivot makes the pitch drift upward a little at each step (comma drift).
- Small drifts add up to a whole new tuning frame, so it is a change of tuning system, not just of key, and every local step still sounds in tune.

## 2. Negative harmony

**Model answer:** Negative harmony reflects every pitch in a key across an axis halfway between the tonic and the fifth (in C, between E-flat and E). Major chords become minor, rising lines become falling, and a dominant like G7 becomes something like Fm6 or Dm7♭5, which pulls to C from the "plagal" side. The idea comes from Ernst Levy's polarity theory and older dualist theory (Zarlino, Riemann's undertone series). Steve Coleman coined the term, and it reached Collier through his teacher Barak Schmool. Collier mostly does not apply it as a strict note-by-note mirror. He treats it as a "polarity" between the overtone and undertone series, a way to reach the tonic from below with darker, wistful colors instead of the usual V–I gravity.

**Key points for full credit:**
- The mechanism: reflection across an axis between root and fifth, which swaps major and minor and flips direction. Dominant function maps to minor-subdominant (plagal) colors.
- The lineage: Levy's polarity theory and earlier dualism, the term from Steve Coleman, then Schmool to Collier. Collier popularized it but did not invent it.
- How Collier uses it: as a color and alternative to ordinary chord motion (a plagal or undertone "gravity"), not as a strict system applied to whole pieces.

**Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_harmony (Collier's "polarity" framing: https://rosetta.to/u/wired/jacob-collier-explains-music-in-5-levels-of-difficulty-ft-herbie-hancock-wired?t=0)

## 3. Brightness and the "Super-Ultra-Hyper-Mega-Meta Lydian"

**Model answer:** The modes of the major scale can be ordered by how many of their notes sit on the sharp (clockwise) side of the circle of fifths from the tonic. Lydian has the most because its raised 4th is one more fifth up, so it is the brightest. Locrian is the darkest. Moving clockwise, by adding successive fifths or key centers, makes the sound brighter, and moving counter-clockwise makes it darker ("fifths imply major, fourths imply minor"). His "Super-Ultra-Hyper-Mega-Meta Lydian" keeps going up the fifths past ordinary Lydian. For arranging, this replaces the happy/sad split of major and minor with a continuous spectrum. He can brighten or darken a passage in fine steps, often by changing one note at a time, to shape emotion precisely.

**Key points for full credit:**
- Brightness follows the circle of fifths: modes from Lydian (brightest, ♯4) to Locrian (darkest). Clockwise (sharpward) motion means brighter, counter-clockwise means darker.
- "Super Lydian" extends that sharpward stacking of fifths beyond the seven-note mode.
- The practical payoff is a continuous emotional dial for reharmonizing and arranging, not a binary major/minor choice.

**Source:** https://www.jazzadvice.com/lessons/the-secret-to-unlocking-the-lydian-sound-in-jazz-improvisation/ (also https://ethanhein.substack.com/p/modes-from-light-to-dark-around-the-chromatic-circle)

## 4. "Every melody note works with every bass note"

**Model answer:** A melody note has no fixed meaning by itself. Its role (root, 3rd, 9th, ♯11, ♭9 and so on) depends on the bass and chord underneath it. So any melody note can sit over any bass note if the voicing and voice leading justify it. In reharmonization Collier keeps the familiar melody and changes what is underneath it, which changes the note's function and emotional color. The usual approach is backwards: it starts from "which diatonic chord contains this note" and finds only a few options. Collier starts from the bass and treats the whole chromatic range as available. He also stresses getting new colors by rearranging the notes you already have (inversions, re-voicing), for example to "delay the gratification of going home."

**Key points for full credit:**
- The melody note's function and color is set by its relation to the bass and the chord. Changing the bass recontextualizes the same pitch.
- Reharmonization means holding the melody constant and freely choosing bass and chord, with all 12 bass notes possible. The conventional approach limits the choice to diatonic chords that contain the note.
- Voicing and inversion (rearranging existing notes) do as much work as adding notes.

**Source:** https://rosetta.to/u/wired/jacob-collier-explains-music-in-5-levels-of-difficulty-ft-herbie-hancock-wired?t=0

## 5. How the vocal stacks are built, and why by hand

**Model answer:** Collier overdubs himself part by part. His early split-screen videos showed him singing each line of the arrangement. The habit comes from singing Bach chorales with his family and from choral singing (Britten), so each layer is a voice-led line in close harmony, not a block chord copied across tracks. Sessions run to hundreds of tracks (*In My Room* was made in his bedroom, largely on one SM58, and often went past Logic's old track limit). He avoids Auto-Tune and Flex Pitch and adjusts pitch by hand (Logic's Time and Pitch Machine, or microtonally following other singers, as in #IHarmU). He also prefers human timing to quantizing. The reason is that his harmony depends on intonation set by context, like pure chords and microtonal drift, plus the "human element." Snapping everything to a 12-TET grid would destroy both.

**Key points for full credit:**
- Stacks are built from individually sung, voice-led lines in close harmony, rooted in chorale and choral singing. They are not duplicated or pitch-shifted copies.
- Pitch is set by ear or adjusted by hand (no Auto-Tune or Flex), and harmonies may move microtonally to fit the context or another singer.
- Timing is left human rather than quantized. Both choices protect the feel and the context-dependent tuning his harmony relies on.

**Source:** https://www.soundonsound.com/people/jacob-collier (background on chorales and split-screen: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Collier)

## 6. Recreating the harmony live: Harmonizer and audience choir

**Model answer:** The Harmonizer, built with Ben Bloomberg of the MIT Media Lab, is a MIDI-triggered vocal harmonizer with 12 voices. Collier sings one note and plays a chord on a keyboard, and the system re-pitches his voice to every note he holds, so his sung note and vowel must fit the chord in real time. A freeze pedal holds chords, and Ableton loopers layer parts, so one person can rebuild a many-part arrangement on stage. The audience choir takes the same idea to the crowd. He splits the room into about three (sometimes up to six) sections, gives each a pitch, and conducts them by gesture, moving lines independently and even into chromatic "in-between" notes. He composes the voice leading in his head as he goes.

**Key points for full credit:**
- The Harmonizer takes his voice as the source and a keyboard (MIDI) for the pitches. It has 12-voice polyphony, so he must keep track of how his sung note sits against the chord he plays.
- The audience choir has sections given starting pitches and steered by hand signals into moving, voice-led harmony, improvised live.
- Both turn the one-man multitrack (many voice-led parts) into something performed in real time.

**Source:** https://www.soundonsound.com/people/jacob-collier (audience choir: https://newatlas.com/music/jacob-collier-audience-choir/)

## 7. The organizing principle of *Djesse*

**Model answer:** Collier organized *Djesse* around acoustic space, not genre. Vol. 1 is a broad, open room with orchestra (Metropole Orkest). Vol. 2 is a smaller, intimate acoustic space suited to folk and jazz. Vol. 3 is "negative space," where the room disappears into electronic, R&B and hip-hop sound design. Vol. 4 gathers the earlier "seeds" into one sound, including huge crowd-choir voices. Because each volume is a different sonic world, the challenge he named was cohesion: making it "feel like one continuous story and not just a jumble of different worlds." He compares his production role to a film director's: making very different elements coexist and cutting the "extraneous rubbish."

**Key points for full credit:**
- The organizing axis is space or room size (large open room, then small room, then negative space, then synthesis), not genre.
- The central challenge is cohesion and a through-line across very different sonic worlds.
- He sees production as directing: getting elements to coexist and editing out the excess.

**Source:** https://www.avclub.com/grammy-winning-virtuoso-jacob-collier-discusses-djesse-1844663166 (cohesion quote: https://www.mixonline.com/recording/jacob-collier-making-elements-co-exist)

## 8. The main critique and its trade-offs

**Model answer:** Sympathetic critics say harmonic display can crowd out the song. In Stevie Wonder's "Don't You Worry 'Bout a Thing," the descending progression keeps a steady top voice: each chord's 3rd becomes the next chord's suspended 4th and then resolves, which Ethan Hein calls "elegant and obvious." Collier's reharmonization moves roots by thirds and adds unusual extensions. It is clever and smooth but weaker, because the simple voice-leading logic is lost. Hein argues that Collier "pays too much attention to the chords" and too little to sound, groove and production. Other critics (the NYT's "strangely banal songs," Rolling Stone Italia) say the music is full of tension but light on resolution and emotional directness. The trade-off is that his density and maximalism produce astonishing moments but can blur a song's core idea and its connection with listeners.

**Key points for full credit:**
- The critique: harmonic complexity and maximalism become the subject of the music at the expense of songwriting, groove, sound and emotional directness.
- The concrete example: Stevie's original works through a simple, clear voice-leading device (3rd becomes sus4 and resolves over a held top voice). Collier's denser reharmonization (roots in thirds, far-out extensions) loses that clarity.
- A balanced answer: the critics grant his technique, and the issue is a trade-off of complexity against clarity and impact, not a lack of skill.
