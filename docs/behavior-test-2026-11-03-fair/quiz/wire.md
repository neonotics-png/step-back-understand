# The Wire: Comprehension Quiz

1. David Simon calls The Wire a Greek tragedy rather than a Shakespearean or conventional TV drama. What plays the role of the "gods" in his version, and how does that change what happens to characters who challenge the way things are, compared with most TV dramas?

2. Each season adds a new Baltimore institution (the drug trade and police, then the port, city politics, schools, and the newspaper) instead of going deeper into the original cops-and-dealers story. What is this structure meant to build up over five seasons? Use Season 2's dockworkers to explain why the move away from the drug corners is not a detour.

3. "Juking the stats" starts as a police term but comes back in other seasons. Explain what it means and how it shows up outside the police department. What does it reveal about why the show's institutions fail even when the people in them are not especially corrupt?

4. The show presents the police department and the Barksdale drug organization as parallel institutions. Name two concrete ways they mirror each other, and explain why that parallel matters more to the show's argument than a "good cops vs. bad criminals" setup would.

5. In Season 3, Major Colvin's "Hamsterdam" free zones and Stringer Bell's attempt to run the drug trade like a legitimate business both end in failure. What does Simon say these two storylines have in common, and why does Hamsterdam end even though it was working by its own measures?

6. Simon says he is "cynical about institutions" but not about individuals. Given that the finale's closing montage shows new people stepping into old roles, is The Wire simply fatalistic? Explain how the show holds both ideas at once, with at least one example.

7. The Wire explains very little: slang and police procedure go undefined, music comes almost entirely from sources inside the scene, and episodes work like chapters rather than self-contained stories. Explain how these formal choices serve the show's argument instead of being only a matter of style.

8. Why does a show about the American city end its run with a season about a newspaper? Explain what the Baltimore Sun storyline says about the press as an institution, how it connects to the show's other themes, and the main criticism critics made of that season.
