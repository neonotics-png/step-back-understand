# The Wire: Answer Key

## 1. Greek tragedy and institutions as gods

**Model answer:** In Simon's framing, the gods are modern institutions: the police department, the drug economy, the political structures and the schools. They throw the lightning bolts for reasons that have little to do with any one character's merit. Most TV drama lets an individual rise above an institution for a cathartic victory. In The Wire, "the institutions always prove larger," and characters who defy them are mocked, marginalized or crushed. So the drama comes from people pushing against forces that do not care about them, not from heroes winning.

**Key points for full credit:**
- The "Olympian forces" are institutions (police, drug trade, politics, schools), not fate or individual villains.
- This reverses the usual TV pattern of individual catharsis or redemption: when people challenge the system, the system wins.
- (Strong answers) The institutions are indifferent rather than evil, so tragedy comes from structure, not personal wickedness.

**Sources:** https://www.thebeliever.net/?p=41916 (Nick Hornby's interview with David Simon, The Believer, 2007); https://freshairarchive.org/segments/david-simon-end-his-wire

## 2. Season-by-season expansion and the "death of work"

**Model answer:** Simon's plan was to "slice off another piece of the American city" each season, so that by the end a simulated Baltimore would stand in for urban America as a whole. Each new institution shows another way the same city fails its people. Simon called Season 2 "a meditation on the death of work and the betrayal of the American working class." When the port's union jobs disappear, the dockworkers turn to smuggling, and that ties straight back to Season 1: with legitimate work gone, the drug trade becomes the only industry left hiring in much of the city.

**Key points for full credit:**
- The goal is cumulative: a whole-city, systemic portrait in which the institutions are linked, not a crime story with side plots.
- Season 2 is about deindustrialization ("the death of work"). Losing blue-collar jobs connects the white working class to the same economic abandonment behind the drug corners.
- (Strong answers) This is "the America that got left behind" (Simon). The drug economy fills the space where legitimate work used to be.

**Sources:** https://www.thebeliever.net/?p=41916 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wire (Simon's quote on Season 2) ; https://www.pbs.org/moyers/journal/04172009/transcript1.html

## 3. "Juking the stats"

**Model answer:** Juking the stats means manipulating the numbers an institution is judged by, "making robberies into larcenies, making rapes disappear," so commanders look good and get promoted. In Season 4, Prez, a former cop turned teacher, sees the same thing in the school as it teaches to the standardized test, and the idea comes back with Burrell's crime numbers and the newspaper's prize-chasing. The point is that institutions reward people for hitting a measurable target instead of doing the actual job. Ordinary careerists end up betraying the institution's real purpose without anyone needing to be a villain. As Simon put it, "statistics will always lie" once a career depends on them.

**Key points for full credit:**
- Definition: gaming performance metrics (reclassifying crimes, teaching to the test) to make numbers look better instead of improving results.
- It appears across institutions: police, schools (Prez in Season 4), City Hall, and arguably the newspaper.
- Why it matters: failure is systemic and driven by incentives. Rational self-interest inside a metric-driven bureaucracy, not personal evil, empties out the mission.

**Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Know_Your_Place ; https://www.pbs.org/moyers/journal/04172009/transcript1.html

## 4. Police and drug organization as mirror institutions

**Model answer:** Both are hierarchies that demand loyalty, protect the people at the top, and sacrifice the people at the bottom. D'Angelo and Wallace are expendable to the Barksdales, just as McNulty is exiled to the marine unit for embarrassing his bosses. Both put appearances and self-preservation ahead of their stated goals, through chain-of-command politics on one side and Stringer's business-style meetings on the other. The parallel matters because it moves the moral focus from individual guilt to how institutions work. Cops and dealers are both pieces in systems that treat their own people the same way, so the show cannot be a simple good-versus-evil story.

**Key points for full credit:**
- At least two concrete parallels, for example: chain of command and politics; loyal or talented members betrayed or sacrificed (Wallace, D'Angelo, McNulty, Daniels); the "pawns" logic of D'Angelo's chess scene; both "juke" or protect their image; Stringer running the crew like a corporation.
- The parallel undercuts the usual good-versus-evil crime framing: both sides are institutions that betray the people who serve them.
- (Strong answers) The moral weight sits in systems, so "bad guys" get full humanity and "good guys" get no exemption.

**Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wire (Simon on institutions betraying their members) ; https://freshairarchive.org/segments/david-simon-end-his-wire ("there's every possibility it will betray you")

## 5. Hamsterdam and Stringer Bell as failed reformers

**Model answer:** Simon said, "Stringer and Colvin are both from different sides trying to reform the drug war, and it's unreformable. It belongs to the gangsters and to the career cops who want to get paid." By its own measures Hamsterdam worked: felonies in the Western District fell sharply and officers could go back to real police work. It was shut down once the press found out, because of political fallout and a threat to federal funding, not because it failed, and Colvin was demoted and made the scapegoat. Both stories show that the system protects its own logic and the people it pays, and gets rid of anyone who tries to change it, whichever side of the law they are on.

**Key points for full credit:**
- Colvin and Stringer are mirror-image reformers (police side and gang side), and both are destroyed. Simon calls the drug war "unreformable."
- Hamsterdam ended for political and institutional reasons (press exposure, the mayor's career, federal money, the brass protecting themselves), not because it failed at reducing crime.
- (Strong answers) This ties to Simon's view that the drug war has become a "war on the underclass" that serves the institutions involved.

**Sources:** https://www.mentalfloss.com/posts/idris-elba-unhappy-stringer-bell-fate-the-wire ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_%22Bunny%22_Colvin ; https://www.pbs.org/moyers/journal/04172009/transcript1.html

## 6. Cynical about institutions, not individuals

**Model answer:** The show is fatalistic about institutions, not about people. In the finale montage, Valchek becomes commissioner, Carcetti wins the governorship, Templeton wins a Pulitzer, Dukie slides into addiction, and Michael becomes a stickup man in the Omar mold. That shows the system reproducing its roles no matter who fills them. Yet individual choices still matter morally and personally. Bubbles gets clean and finally sits down to eat with his sister, Colvin takes in Namond and changes his life, and Carver grows into a conscientious officer. The show insists that decency is real and worth something, while showing that individual decency does not fix the structure.

**Key points for full credit:**
- The finale's replacement or cycle imagery shows institutions outlasting individuals and recasting the same roles.
- Individuals still have moral agency and can be saved on a small scale (Bubbles, Namond and Colvin, Carver), so the show is not nihilistic.
- The distinction: personal redemption is possible, but systemic redemption by heroic individuals is not. That is the opposite of the usual TV formula.

**Sources:** https://www.pbs.org/moyers/journal/04172009/transcript1.html ("I am very cynical about institutions... I am not cynical when it comes to individuals") ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/-30-_(The_Wire)

## 7. Form serving the argument

**Model answer:** Simon's rule was "fuck the average reader": he refused to slow the story down for exposition, on the view that TV is "a condescending medium, explaining everything immediately." Dropping viewers into undefined slang and procedure makes them learn the world the way an outsider would, which makes it feel like a real place with its own logic. Built like a multi-POV novel, the show rewards long attention and lets causes and effects play out across seasons. That suits an argument about slow, systemic forces better than episodic case-of-the-week plots that resolve in an hour. Diegetic music, real Baltimore locations and actual local figures cut out the emotional cues and gloss of a typical drama, which supports the show's claim to describe how things really work.

**Key points for full credit:**
- Withholding exposition trusts the viewer and immerses them. Meaning builds up instead of being handed over (Simon's "fuck the average reader" or anti-condescension stance).
- The novelistic, cumulative structure (episodes as chapters, "all the pieces matter") matches the systemic argument. Problems do not resolve in one episode because systems don't.
- (Strong answers) Realism choices (diegetic music, local non-actors and real figures, composites of real people, Simon and Burns's reporting and police backgrounds) avoid melodramatic manipulation and support the claim to verisimilitude.

**Sources:** https://www.thebeliever.net/?p=41916 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wire

## 8. Season 5 and the newspaper

**Model answer:** The press is supposed to be the institution that notices when the others fail. Season 5 shows the Sun, weakened by buyouts and run by prize-hungry editors, missing the systemic story and chasing a "Dickensian" human-interest angle instead, while Templeton fabricates and is rewarded with a Pulitzer. That is the newspaper's version of juking the stats, and it mocks the very "Dickensian" label critics had been giving the show, since Dickens-style stories favor individual pathos over structural causes. Many critics who had praised the show called this its weakest season. They saw the newsroom villains as broad caricatures and the storyline as Simon settling scores with his former employer.

**Key points for full credit:**
- The press's job is to explain the systemic failures of the other institutions. Its failure means nobody is watching, which completes the portrait of the city.
- Connection to recurring themes: prize-chasing and fabrication are a form of juking the stats, and the "Dickensian aspect" satire rejects individual-pathos storytelling in favor of systemic causes.
- The critical reception: seen as the weakest season, with caricatured villains (Templeton, the editors) and a perceived grudge against the Baltimore Sun.

**Sources:** https://reason.com/?p=126028 ; https://www.huffpost.com/entry/the-wire-dickens_b_5549385 ; https://www.pbs.org/moyers/journal/04172009/transcript1.html (Simon on beat reporters no longer "looking for the systemic")
