Subject and question:
- Subject: Test-driven development as Kent Beck formulated it in *Test-Driven Development: By Example* (Addison-Wesley; the sample pages carry the production stamp "BECK.book ... Thursday, October 10, 2002"), restated by Beck in "Canon TDD" (2023-12-11).
- Representative formulation (Preface, verbatim): "In Test-Driven Development, we • Write new code only if an automated test has failed • Eliminate duplication". "The two rules imply an order to the tasks of programming. 1. Red—Write a little test that doesn't work, and perhaps doesn't even compile at first. 2. Green—Make the test work quickly, committing whatever sins necessary in the process. 3. Refactor—Eliminate all of the duplication created in merely getting the test to work. Red/green/refactor—the TDD mantra."
- Open question: where did (a) the write-the-test-first rule and (b) the red-green-refactor loop come from? Which parts did Beck inherit, which did he assemble, and when?

What this reveals about the subject:
- "Test first" and "red-green-refactor" have different parents. The rule is old and inherited: Beck says, three times across 24 years, that he read it in a punched-tape-era book as a child and only recognised it after he had built SUnit (1994). The loop's vocabulary is young and borrowed: "red" and "green" are the JUnit progress bar of 1997–98, and "refactor" is the Cunningham/WyCash/Opdyke/Fowler lineage. The 2002 book is where the three words are first (as far as this inquiry can show) chained into "the TDD mantra".
- Beck's own dating is tighter than the folklore. By his account the chain is: SUnit for a consulting client (1994) → demo to Ward at OOPSLA Austin (Oct 1995) → JUnit written test-first on the OOPSLA flight (Oct 1997) → "write the tests first" in print in Test Infected (Jul 1998) → XP Explained (1999) → *TDD: By Example* (Oct 2002). The Wikipedia "SUnit 1989" date is contradicted by Cambridge's "October, 1994" and by Beck himself.
- The second rule, "Eliminate duplication", is what makes the loop a design method rather than a testing method. Its ancestry is the Smalltalk refactoring culture, and the WyCash story in the Introduction is Beck's chosen proof that tests at the right level let a design be pared down and then extended in two days.
- TDD was framed by Beck as a portable substitute for an environment: "an attempt to give any software engineer, working in any environment, the sense of comfort and intimacy we had with our Smalltalk environment". That explains design choices that look arbitrary from outside: tests must be programmer-written and run "20 times per day"; steps must be tiny; the tool must answer in seconds.
- The rule was already in practice in the late 1950s (Mercury, per Larman & Basili and Weinberg), which supports Beck's "rediscovery" wording without implying any transmission to him.

What remains distinctive:
- The emotional framing. Beck's stated motivation is "Courage": "Test-driven development is a way of managing fear during programming"; "The tests in test-driven development are the teeth of the ratchet." No earlier source in this inquiry frames testing this way.
- "Green" permits sin. "Make the test work quickly, committing whatever sins necessary in the process" deliberately separates making-it-pass from making-it-right; the cleanup is a scheduled phase, not an afterthought. Test Infected (1998) has the tests and the refactoring but not this licence.
- The loop is presented as a mantra to be repeated minute by minute, with the duplication rule as the design engine, and with the book itself written as a transcript of a pair-programming session ("I wrote the examples imagining a pair programming session").
- Beck keeps the practice "fuzzier" than XP: "TDD is an awareness of the gap between decision and feedback during programming, and techniques to control that gap." In Canon TDD (2023) he adds a test list up front and makes refactoring explicitly optional ("Optionally refactor to improve the implementation design"; "Duplication is a hint, not a command"), which softens the 2002 third step.

Unresolved branches:
- The identity of the "ancient" tape-era book. Beck calls its author "unknown"; no source names it. Candidates from that era are not evidenced, so none is proposed.
- The full text of "Simple Smalltalk Testing: With Patterns" could not be reached (xprogramming.com refused connections; the Czech mirror's DNS failed; archive.org is blocked for this tool; Scribd shows only a summary). Whether the 1994 column itself says "write the test first" is therefore unverified; the earliest direct "write the tests first" sentence I read is Test Infected (1998).
- Beck's Quora answer (2012) was quoted via Wikipedia, not read at source (Quora returned 403 and the archive copy was blocked).
- Whether anyone used the phrase "red/green/refactor" before the 2002 book. Fowler says the three steps are "often summarized as Red - Green - Refactor" but gives no origin; Wikipedia's sentence on the mantra is uncited.
- The refactoring step's attribution rests partly on Mike Beedle's XpRoots account and on second-hand descriptions of the "two hats" in Fowler's *Refactoring*; Fowler's text was not fetched.
- Larman & Basili's Mercury paragraph exists in two circulating wordings ("half-day" in the PDF read here; "n-day" in another copy per search results); the primary Mercury documentation was not examined.
- The 2025 Pragmatic Engineer podcast transcript (the "Genesis and Emotional Impact of TDD" segment at 35:32) was behind a sign-in; its claim that SUnit was built at MasPar for a consulting client rests on a third-party summary and is not used above.

What changes for the current task (if any):
- No current task beyond understanding. What the reader can now recognise: when someone says "Beck invented TDD", the accurate version is that Beck inherited the test-first rule from a 1950s/60s-era text, built the tool (SUnit, then JUnit with Gamma) that made it cheap to follow, took the refactoring leg and the WyCash proof from Cunningham's Smalltalk practice, took the red/green words from the JUnit bar, and in 2002 chained them into a named loop with a fear-management rationale and a duplication rule that no single predecessor had.
