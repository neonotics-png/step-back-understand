# Why Khruangbin sounds the way it does

Khruangbin (Thai for "airplane") is Mark Speer (guitar), Laura Lee Ochoa (bass) and Donald "DJ" Johnson (drums). The short version: the sound is what you get when a Houston church-band guitarist obsessed with 1970s cassettes from Thailand, Iran and Nigeria teaches a math teacher to play bass using dub reggae and Serge Gainsbourg records, recruits a hip-hop producer who plays organ in the same gospel band, and then records all of it live in a dirt-floored barn with no isolation. Each of the three things you noticed (reverb-heavy guitar, bass-led grooves, few vocals) traces back to a specific person, place or record. I'm working from interviews and reporting, not from listening.

## 1. Houston: the melting pot, the church, and hip-hop

**The gospel band.** Speer and Johnson met in Houston in 2004 playing in Pastor Rudy Rasmus's gospel band at St. John's Methodist Church; the church hired Speer as guitarist and Johnson as organist. Several accounts say they first crossed paths at the Red Cat Jazz Cafe and only played together a few years later. Either way, the foundation the band shares, in Lee's words, is "R&B, gospel, and 'transportive' music." She has argued that gospel, while not psychedelic as a genre, has the same ability to carry a listener away that psych rock does. That explains a lot: Khruangbin's songs are built to lift and hold a mood rather than to climax, which is a church-band skill.

**Houston hip-hop.** Before Khruangbin, Johnson was half of Beanz N Kornbread, a production duo that made tracks for Houston rap artists like Slim Thug, Paul Wall and Z-Ro. That is the drummer's instinct: he plays "simple breakbeats on a simple kit, with no fills or solos," and says "the music tells you what it needs." Speer himself says his first love was hip-hop (he cites Grandmaster Flash's "The Message") and that he initially disliked rock; he picked up guitar to play big fusion-style jazz chords, not power chords. So two of the three members came to the band thinking like beatmakers, not rock musicians.

**The city itself.** Johnson describes Houston as "a bit of a melting pot" where many cultures blend, and says that shapes the art made there. Speer's background also included Houston's dance-music and prog-influenced jazz-funk scene. None of this makes Khruangbin a "Houston-sounding" band in the ZZ Top or chopped-and-screwed sense; what Houston gave them was a gospel groove discipline, a hip-hop production mindset, and a comfort with being a mix of things.

## 2. The cassette blog: why the "Thai funk" label stuck

Speer found a blog called Monrakplengthai ("I love Thai music"), made entirely of uploads of old Thai cassettes from the 1960s to 1980s. He and Lee dove in. Speer says he was hunting for "funky" music from far away, roughly 1977 to 1983, from Nigeria, Thailand and the Philippines. The Thai material included luk thung and molam (Thai country genres), electric molam, and Thai bands influenced by The Shadows, the British instrumental guitar group. He found the Sutrak Akson-thong tape *Rak Rop Ramphan* through the blog and later tracked down physical copies in Thailand.

What he took from it was not licks. "I'm not pressing rewind to learn licks and phrases," he said; what he liked was that "in luuk thung and molam the changes don't line up as you'd expect." Their debut *The Universe Smiles Upon You* (2015) drew on 1960s luk thung; the band name came from Lee learning Thai with a bootleg Rosetta Stone while Speer played Thai cassettes as she cooked.

Both of them push back on the "Thai funk" tag. Johnson says "we're not a Thai band" and that actual Thai funk groups like Paradise Bangkok "sound nothing like what we do." Speer's own description: "I will borrow everything." By the second album, *Con Todo El Mundo* (2018), the center had moved to Iranian pop (Googoosh especially), plus Middle Eastern, North African, Spanish and Irish music; Speer says the scales and grooves on that record are largely Persian. *Ali* (2022) was a collaboration with Vieux Farka Touré playing songs by his father Ali Farka Touré. The common thread is 1970s session-band music from everywhere; the band has named the Wrecking Crew, Muscle Shoals, the Funk Brothers, Ethiopia's Roha Band, Sly and Robbie, and Kassav' as models.

## 3. The reverb-heavy guitar: a singer's role, a three-piece's problem, and a barn

Three separate reasons stack up here.

**The guitar replaces the singer.** Speer: in Khruangbin, "the guitar is taking the place of the lead singer." Because of that he listens to more singers than guitarists and is "specifically trying to make it sing." He is drawn to music in languages he does not understand, because then all he hears is "an amazing melody delivered in a captivating way." Interviewers have noted he mimics vocal phrasing of Thai singer Dao Bandon and Indian playback singer Lata Mangeshkar. A guitar playing vocal lines is naturally a legato, sustained, reverb-friendly guitar.

**Filling out a trio.** "There's only three of us, so I'm using ambient effects like reverb and delay to fill it out." His live chain is almost absurdly simple: a 2001 Stratocaster (nearly the only guitar he owns), flatwound strings with an unwound G because "I just don't like the way new strings sound," a wah left on, a Boss DS-1, and an Electro-Harmonix Holy Grail on the spring setting. He uses the pedal instead of amp reverb because he never knows what amp he'll get abroad. The flatwounds and the deliberately dull, "budget Teisco"-like wiring of the Strat are why the tone reads as old rather than modern.

**The sound of the instruments he loves.** He ties his reverb taste to the sitar and the rubab (Afghan lute), which have sympathetic strings and a naturally reverberant quality that creates a "mystical kind of vibe." Lee and Speer first bonded over Afghan music and Middle Eastern architecture. He prefers mechanical reverbs with "a lot of weird character," and says "we use springs a lot." That spring-reverb sound also connects to the Shadows-style Thai bands and to surf; critics describe his playing as part Dick Dale, part late-period George Harrison.

**The barn.** The band formed and still records in a late-1800s barn on Speer's family property in Burton, Texas (population about 300, little cell service, no Wi-Fi), an hour and a half from Houston. It has dirt floors and corrugated tin siding; opening the doors gives a drier sound, closing them adds room. There is no isolation, so everything bleeds. Engineer Steve Christensen puts dynamic mics near the players, records in mono, and the three play live in a circle facing each other, no punch-ins; overdubs are done afterwards in Houston. Speer even clapped in the barn, recorded it, and made an impulse response they use during mixing, and he keeps the outdoor ambience (birds, wind in the grass) in. His line: the sound "was born in a barn," and "we don't like studios." They chose the barn originally because it was free and Houston studios were expensive. So part of the "reverb" you hear is simply the room.

## 4. The bass-led grooves: dub, Gainsbourg, and a beginner's limitations

**Lee was a beginner, and that is the point.** She was a math teacher who met Speer in 2007 through friends, started playing his bass whenever they hung out, and had him feed her records to learn from. He bought her an SX Jazz Bass knock-off in college and swapped the pickups for DiMarzio humbuckers; she still plays it. She practiced through a stretch of insomnia, and after about six months Speer got her an audition for a 2010 US tour with Yppah, opening for Bonobo. After the tour she quit her job and asked to start a band. Speer brought in Johnson, and they recorded an EP in the barn before ever playing a show (first gig: Fitzgerald's, Houston, 2011).

She has no formal training and considers that a feature. She plays with two left-hand fingers tied together, compares herself to James Jamerson's one-finger style, and says "if you lose the fourth finger thing, it creates a limitation, but for me it's fun and bouncy." Her motto: "all pocket and no chops" beats the reverse.

**Dub as her "ABCs."** She says she learned to play bass to dub reggae records, calling dub her "ABCs of music" because the patterns were simple enough for a beginner to follow. One of the first lines she learned was Barrington Levy's "When Friday Comes," and she has described the Scientist dub versions as the thing that taught her how parts sit in a mix. Her feel, she says, comes from dub; she also names Jamerson and McCartney. Dub is a bass-and-drums music with everything else drifting in and out of reverb, which is close to a description of Khruangbin.

**Gainsbourg as her tone.** Her favorite album is *Jane Birkin/Serge Gainsbourg* (1969), which Speer gave her when they met. She calls its bass "my ideal bass" and says "I'm always trying to get the tone on that record"; her tone, she says, comes from the session players on Gainsbourg's records. The band also says that album taught them about playing quietly and dynamics. She has said Birkin's innocence gave her a model for how naivety can be a strength when she felt insecure next to bandmates who had played all their lives. She runs her bass through a Fender Bassman 10, which Speer notes does not scoop the mids the way modern bass amps do, so the bass sits forward as a melodic voice.

**The writing process literally starts with drums and bass.** The songs are written to loops. In the early days Speer made Lee a bank of hip-hop drum loops; she wrote bass lines to the breaks, he wrote guitar on top, and the tracks went to Johnson. Later it was often Johnson sending loops. Speer's summary: "It's all bass and drums, and I adapt guitar changes to the basslines." Relix describes the early demos as "built around Speer's looped breakbeats," drenched in the Thai funk and soul ethos. When the guitar is written last, to fit the bass, the bass leads by construction. Lee adds that the musical parts are always written first, no matter the record, and that songs are composed in "jam form," recorded live, edited on a computer, then relearned as fixed arrangements: "It's not just us jamming in a room."

## 5. Why mostly instrumental

Speer's answer is personal: he avoided singing because "I don't really dig that vibe" and did not want to be a singer stuck behind a mic. He grew up listening to wordless music (dub, classical, jazz) while studying, and he thinks titles give the listener words to think about while wordless tracks leave room for their own stories. The structural reason follows: if the guitar is the singer, you do not need another one. Lee adds that starting as an instrumental act meant "every part is equally weighted," and the band has said they are "trying to make a big sound with a small amount of people," where "sometimes one note's all you need."

The music was never entirely voiceless; the band's early vocal approach was modeled on 1970s War and Santana records, where voices are group chants inside the groove. When *Mordechai* (2020) put vocals on nearly every track, they kept that logic: most songs have all three singing in unison. Lee: "We have no interest in doing vocal acrobatics." Speer: "If you're all just singing together, it just sounds like a bunch of people having a good time." The voice became another instrument rather than a front person. The band has also framed the whole project as "the absence of that aggression that a lot of rock bands have."

## 6. How it got out of the barn

Lee moved to London in 2013, handed Bonobo a CD, and "A Calf Born in Winter," the band's first recording, ended up on his *Late Night Tales* compilation that year. It became one of the record's most popular tracks and got them a deal with the Late Night Tales label. Later: *Con Todo El Mundo* (2018, title from Lee's grandfather), *Mordechai* (2020, Dead Oceans), two EPs with Leon Bridges (*Texas Sun*, *Texas Moon*), *Ali* (2022), *A La Sala* (2024), a Best New Artist Grammy nomination in late 2024, and a reworked *The Universe Smiles Upon You II* (2025).

## The one-paragraph answer

A Houston band sounds like this because Houston gave them gospel groove and a hip-hop producer's loop-first habits; a cassette blog gave Speer a library of 1970s Thai, Iranian and West African session-band music whose melodies he decided the guitar should "sing"; dub and a Gainsbourg record gave a self-taught math teacher a bass style that is all pocket and no chops; the writing method (bass to a breakbeat, guitar fitted last) makes the bass lead by construction; a dirt-floored barn with no isolation, spring reverb and dynamic mics in mono gives everything its haze; and a guitarist who never wanted to be a singer made the guitar the singer, so there was no job left for words.
