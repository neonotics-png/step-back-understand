# Mon Rovîa and *Bloodline*

Mon Rovîa is the stage name of Janjay Lowe, a singer-songwriter born in Monrovia, Liberia, during the civil war. He never knew his father, and his mother died when he was an infant. Christian missionaries adopted him at around seven, and the family moved through Florida, Montana and the Bahamas before settling near Chattanooga, Tennessee. His adoptive father was a pastor, and the family took in other war orphans as well. *Bloodline* (Nettwerk, January 9, 2026; 16 songs in about 43 minutes, written and recorded in Los Angeles with producer Cooper Holzman) is his first full-length folk album. Wikipedia lists a 2021 hip-hop/R&B/folk project, *Dark Continent*, so calling *Bloodline* his "debut" depends on what you count. The album tells his life story in order: war in Liberia, displacement, growing up Black in a white family in the South, and arriving at a sense of belonging.

I haven't heard the record. The descriptions of how it sounds below come from reviews and interviews.

## What it sounds like, and why

**Soft indie-folk with a ukulele at the center.** The palette is gentle and mostly acoustic: his ukulele, plus guitar, banjo, mandolin, dobro, fiddle and strings, with choirs and piano behind them. His voice is high, quiet and slightly unusual. The lineage explains it. His older foster brothers introduced him to Fleet Foxes, Bon Iver and Radical Face, and he has said "the love of music kind of was formed there." Those bands share a recipe: hushed, high vocals, stacked harmonies that sound like a small choir, and folk instruments recorded with a lot of room around them. Reviewers hear early Bon Iver on the title track, and that is the inheritance. The ukulele is his own. His parents gave him one shortly before COVID, and during lockdown he went back to "just writing on the ukulele." So a lot of the record is built on a small, bright instrument rather than the usual folk guitar.

He came to that sound late. As a teenager he listened to Kendrick Lamar and Lil Baby and made "sing-song rap" and R&B, partly because he saw almost no Black artists in indie-folk. He has said he thought of folk "as a white experience" and didn't feel he could make it. The spoken-word passages on *Bloodline* ("Whose Face Am I" opens with one, and the closer is a spoken epilogue) may carry some of that earlier phase, along with his poetry and English studies at a college on Lookout Mountain. That last point is my inference; he hasn't said it.

**"Afro-Appalachian" is a historical claim, not just a label.** In interviews he keeps returning to the fact that the banjo descends from West African gourd lutes, and that Appalachian music mixed African, Irish and Scottish traditions. The background makes his old belief that folk was "white" make sense. In the 1920s, record companies sold rural Southern music in two catalogues, white "hillbilly" records and Black "race" records, and the string-band and banjo music went into the white one. Black musicians were written out of a tradition they helped create. When he puts West African melodic turns alongside banjo, fiddle and mandolin, he is reconnecting music that was separated. Rhiannon Giddens has done similar work from the scholarly side. He hasn't cited her, so that is only a parallel. Critics also hear Paul Simon's *Graceland* and Cat Stevens in the brighter songs, and *The Guardian* compared him to Nick Drake and Labi Siffre. Those are critics' comparisons, not his.

## The central tension: a soothing sound under harrowing lyrics

This is what reviewers keep coming back to. *The Guardian* said the "arresting serenity of his music is in deliberate contrast to his harrowing past." Pitchfork described soothing guitar and fiddle clashing with graphic lyrics. "A Day at the Soccer Fields" recalls a weapon pointed at him as a child, over sliding strings. "Running Boy" puts a police encounter and survivor's guilt inside a sing-along chorus, and Pitchfork called it "outright uncomfortable." That discomfort looks intended. The gentle indie-folk sound he learned from his brothers' records is what makes the war memories hit harder. The pairing of that specific sound with that specific story is what's most his own here. Neither Bon Iver nor the Appalachian tradition accounts for it.

The church is the other strand. He grew up in a pastor's home listening mostly to Christian music. The title track is built on found sound from a church service, and one reviewer singles out "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" as the record's clearest blend of gospel and folk.

## The title and the Liberia–America loop

He had the title in mind for years, back when he was working on *Dark Continent*. He describes the project as "a search for that history of myself, and trying to reconnect with pieces that have been lost." For an adoptee who lost both parents early, "bloodline" is the thing that was cut.

Liberian history adds another layer, though he hasn't framed it this way himself. Liberia was founded in the 1820s as a colony for freed Black Americans, many of them from the South, and its capital was named after President James Monroe. So the city he took his name from was itself a product of American history. A boy from Monrovia raised in the American South, singing in a Black Southern tradition that was erased, retraces that route in reverse. Some song titles point back to these roots. *Pray the Devil Back to Hell* is the title of a 2008 documentary about the Liberian women's movement that helped end the second civil war. "Code of Many Colors" echoes Dolly Parton's East Tennessee song "Coat of Many Colors" and the biblical Joseph story it draws on, which is about a favored son taken from his home. I couldn't find him explaining either title, so these are echoes, not confirmed references.

## Why it's structured as short chapters

The songs are short vignettes, and one reviewer counted the brevity as a gift. Before the album he released four EPs titled after stages of the hero's journey: *Act 1: The Wandering*, *Act 2: Trials* (both 2023), *Act 3: The Dying of Self* (2024) and *Act 4: Atonement* (2025). He loves *The Lord of the Rings* for "the smallest of us doing the biggest journey." In Joseph Campbell's version of that arc, atonement comes before the return home. *Bloodline* opens in Liberia and ends "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea," so it plays like that return. That reading is mine, not his. The short songs also fit how he built his audience, through TikTok clips (close to a billion views) and a steady run of singles, before most people had heard an album.

## The protest side

Not everything is memoir. "Heavy Foot" is a sing-along about governments lying to and oppressing people, and it reached No. 3 on the US Triple A radio chart. "Somewhere Down in Georgia" takes on Southern racism. He names James Baldwin, Toni Morrison and Amiri Baraka as the writers who gave him confidence to make this music, and he reads Civil Rights history because he sees parallels with today. He has said he wants to "dedicate it back to my people," meaning Liberia.

## Reception and context

The reviews were strongly positive. Pitchfork praised his "impressive clarity of vision," NME called it "purposeful, resonant folk storytelling," and NPR said his "sound comes full circle." The album entered the Billboard charts, though I couldn't confirm the position. Around it he played the Grand Ole Opry (2025), Bonnaroo, Newport Folk, ACL and Red Rocks, opened for Alabama Shakes, and did an NPR Tiny Desk (September 2026). The main complaints are minor: the spoken epilogue is the one track a reviewer would skip, and some listeners will find the gap between sound and subject unsettling.

One factual discrepancy: an NME piece says he was "rescued from life as a child soldier," but a local profile says the two boys adopted *with* him had fought in the war. I couldn't confirm which is right, so treat the child-soldier detail as uncertain.
