# Mon Rovîa and *Bloodline*

Mon Rovîa (born Janjay Lowe) is a singer-songwriter who was born in Liberia and raised in East Tennessee. He calls his music "Afro-Appalachian folk." *Bloodline* (Nettwerk, January 9, 2026) is his first full-length album: 16 short songs that tell his life story, from the Liberian civil war through adoption to an adult life in the Appalachian foothills. The album does one thing most of all. It sets very hard material (guns, a dead mother, survivor's guilt, police) inside soft, pretty, communal-sounding folk. Almost everything about the record follows from the life behind it and from how he came to folk.

## Who he is

He was born in Monrovia during Liberia's civil wars. His mother died when he was a baby. His grandmother couldn't raise three children, so she placed him with a white American missionary family. He was the only one of his family to get out, and for years he didn't know what had happened to his siblings. He was about seven when he left. Sources disagree on whether that was during the first war or the second, and none give a birth year. The family moved between Florida, Montana, the Bahamas and Tennessee. He went to Christian schools in a very religious home and says he barely heard mainstream pop until high school. He settled in Chattanooga, where he still lives. The stage name is the name of his birth city with an accent added. The question at the center of his work is the one in the Guardian headline: "Why was it me?"

The setting matters. Freed Black Americans founded Liberia in the 1800s, and the capital is named after President James Monroe. So a Liberian child raised in the American South is crossing a line between the two countries that was drawn long before him. In interviews he talks about "the spectre of colonialism in Liberia and the US," and *Bloodline* is built on that two-way link.

## How he got to folk, and why it matters for the sound

He didn't start out as a folk singer. As a teenager he listened mostly to rap (he names Kendrick Lamar and Lil Baby), and his early releases were R&B and hip-hop, up to the 2021 album *Dark Continent*. He has said that as a Black artist he felt expected to make rap, and that he once thought of folk as "a white experience." His older brothers had played him Fleet Foxes and Bon Iver, and he calls Bon Iver his way into music. During COVID he picked up a ukulele, posted simple clips to TikTok at his manager Eric Cromartie's urging, and they spread quickly. From 2023 to 2025 he released four EPs that follow the "hero's journey" story pattern. His favorite story is *The Lord of the Rings*.

Three things about *Bloodline* make sense once you know that path:

- **Soft music, harsh words.** He says the soothing sound is deliberate: it "break[s] down people's edges" so the hard lyrics get through. The vocal style comes from Bon Iver-era indie folk, with light, high, layered voices. Reviewers say a Bon Iver influence is plain on the title track. Critics also compare him to Nick Drake and Labi Siffre, but he says he hadn't heard Drake when listeners first made that comparison, so it's a resemblance, not an influence. One profile says he counts Tracy Chapman among his biggest influences. I found that in only one source, but it fits: Chapman also sang political songs in a quiet, intimate way. Pitchfork describes "soothing guitar and fiddle alongside graphic lyrics." On "Day at the Soccer Fields," sliding strings carry a childhood memory of facing a gun. On "Running Boy," a police encounter breaks into a sing-along chorus. One reviewer found that clash "uncomfortable," and that is the intended effect.
- **Sing-along, church-like choruses.** He grew up with church and private Christian schools, and the Guardian says he "took solace in Mumford & Sons." Both point to big shared choruses. "Heavy Foot," the breakout single (No. 3 on Billboard's Adult Alternative Airplay chart), turns into a group chant and then a bluegrass stomp. NPR's critic put it with the stomp-and-holler style. The title track opens with a recording from a church service.
- **Short songs, polished sound.** His audience came from short TikTok videos, and the album plays like a set of short scenes rather than long folk ballads. He wrote and recorded it in Los Angeles with Cooper Holzman, a California songwriter-producer whose other credits are mostly indie-pop. That probably explains why it sounds radio-ready rather than rough. I'm inferring that from Holzman's other work; nobody has described the sessions.

## What "Afro-Appalachian" means on the record

The term rests on a historical point he makes himself: Appalachian music came partly from West Africa. The banjo descends from West African gourd lutes, and Black musicians mixed with Scots-Irish fiddle music in the mountains. His version of that story is a little more romantic than the record supports, but the basic point is sound. On the album it shows up as texture, not as old-time playing:

- banjo over a steady drum on "Old Fort Steel Trail"
- dobro, mandolin, hand claps and stomping on "Field Song"
- marimba on "Oh Wide World"
- spoken word in Liberian English on "Whose Face Am I"
- plucked strings that one reviewer thought might be a kora on "Infinite Pines"

Reviewers keep naming Paul Simon and *Graceland*. Simon is the clearest earlier example of an American folk record built with African sounds, and one profile says Simon is an influence. The difference is that Simon was an outsider borrowing African sounds. Mon Rovîa uses them to tell the story of his own two homes.

The Liberian references are specific, not general. "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" takes its title from the 2008 documentary about Leymah Gbowee and the Christian and Muslim women whose peace campaign helped end Liberia's second civil war. "Code of Many Colors" sounds like a nod to Dolly Parton's "Coat of Many Colors," the best-known Appalachian song about a poor childhood. He hasn't said that, so it's my guess.

## The politics

He wants to be remembered as an artist who "said something in times when nobody said anything." He names the civil rights movement, James Baldwin and Toni Morrison as influences. "Heavy Foot" is a protest song about poverty, gun violence, propaganda and government power, and Dazed reports that it criticizes US politicians over Palestine. He has headlined an ACLU benefit and played a benefit for children affected by war. Expect this side of him to keep growing. He describes music as "both memoir and medicine," and says he wants to open folk up to young Black listeners.

## What is his own

The influences explain the sound: indie-folk vocals, stomp-and-holler choruses, Simon-style African textures. They don't explain the point of view, which is his alone. He writes as a war survivor raised by the kind of missionaries who came to save him, and he asks who his family was and what his survival cost ("We all long to know who brought us into this world, and at what cost"). Most Americana musicians who reclaim the Black roots of the music are recovering a history that belongs to someone else. He is telling his own.

## Reception and context

The album came out during a wave of popular, socially conscious folk; WXPN compares him to Noah Kahan, Hozier and Jesse Welles. Critics praised it widely. Pitchfork said he approaches his upbringing "with an impressive clarity of vision." NME put him on its cover, and the Guardian and NPR covered him too. One review called the album "confident and competent" and "multi-faceted," and its only real complaint was that the spoken-word closer was unnecessary. Around the album he played the Grand Ole Opry (2025), Newport Folk and an NPR Tiny Desk concert (September 2026). He was also named outstanding artist of the year at Liberia's 2025 music awards.
