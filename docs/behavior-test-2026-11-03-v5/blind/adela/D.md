# ADÉLA, PRIMA: quiz answers (from memory, no lookups)

**1. "KGB"**
In the video ADÉLA plays a spy, a "fly on the wall" who takes in American pop culture from the outside. That fits her position: an Eastern European newcomer studying how US pop works and then working her way into it. The video ends with a disclaimer that the Soviet imagery is "an acknowledgment of where I'm from," not an endorsement, and says she stands with the people of Ukraine. Critics called the track a sardonic, celebratory "firecracker."

**2. "Ain't In LA"**
The closer draws on her mother's youth in communist Czechoslovakia, when Western pop posters were banned. Her grandfather, a miner in West Germany, smuggled them over the border, and a photo of her mother at 18 in front of a wall of Western pop images inspired the song and video. The video was shot in Bratislava while referencing LA and Hollywood. Its message is "You don't have to leave your country," with the line "I'd rather be a nobody than to be like everyone." So the album's drive toward Hollywood stardom ends by treating home and family history as enough, which turns the LA dream back toward Slovakia.

**3. Hard and soft**
She said she wants the album to "sound exactly like a 22-year-old girl," meaning vulnerability next to swagger and humor, and "muscular beats with feminine energy." The upbeat side is tracks like "KGB" or "Boys," a cocky club track with glossy production reminiscent of Missy Elliott and the Neptunes. "Therapy" is the soft side. It is diary-like, written while she was sick on tour, and about her bond with her older sister 6,000 miles away, with some hyperpop touches from Dylan Brady.

**4. Why "PRIMA," and why polished pop**
"Prima" means "first," because this is her debut. It also refers to the prima ballerina, the top dancer in a ballet company. She trained in ballet in Vienna and London as a teen and says she wants to be "the prima ballerina of pop." That goal implies aiming for the very top of mainstream pop, which helps explain the bigger, cleaner sound with Capitol and executive producers Dylan Brady and Blake Slatkin. She called the album a maturation of the sound she built on her own. I don't recall her explicitly explaining why she moved away from the EP's stranger electropop, so that part is my inference.

**5. Frankness about sex and the body**
What I found: the label framed "Fantasize" as sex-positive affirmation and "I'm The Man" and "Boys" as reframing gender roles. The FADER described her provocation as a way to "seize control of her story." She described the choreography as "campy, wink-wink energy, almost ironic," so the cheekiness is partly deliberate camp. I didn't find her own detailed personal explanation for the sexual content specifically, so I can't give more than that without guessing.

**6. The path to PRIMA**
She competed on HYBE x Geffen's *The Debut: Dream Academy*, which formed KATSEYE, and was in the first group eliminated. Netflix's *Pop Star Academy: KATSEYE* (2024) gave her a villain edit and online backlash. She answered by releasing "Homewrecked" independently within weeks, then "Superscar" and the Grimes co-produced "Machine Girl." Capitol signed her in May 2025, and *The Provocateur* EP (Aug 2025) followed, with "DeathByDevotion" and "SexOnTheBeat." That built an image as a self-aware provocateur. *PRIMA* keeps that persona but is more polished and mainstream. Some critics (The Line of Best Fit) found it less adventurous than the EP.

**7. Critical reception**
Reviews were mixed to positive. Clash gave it 8/10 for "palpable star quality." Rolling Stone was positive. The Daily Illini gave it 9/10 and Ratings Game Music a B+. The Line of Best Fit gave it 6/10, and Sputnik gave it 3/5. The main split: admirers heard confident, charismatic, well-sequenced pop, while skeptics called it formulaic and leaning on nostalgia and borrowed styles (Britney, Gwen, Miley, Addison Rae), and weaker than the EP. Both sides liked "Ain't In LA," and "KGB" was also widely praised. "Hitachi" split them: Clash praised the vocal, while The Line of Best Fit called it half-baked.

**8. The "revenge record" expectation**
They wouldn't find a KATSEYE diss album. As far as I found, no track targets the group. The songs are about her ambition, sex and gender play, her family's history behind the Iron Curtain, and her relationship with her sister. That fits how she has handled it: she answered the villain edit by releasing music quickly rather than feuding, called the experience oddly freeing ("If I'm the villain in your story… maybe it helps you feel better"), and some sources say she stayed close with former competitors such as KATSEYE's Lara Raj. Her chart success, where "Nicole Kidman" outcharted any KATSEYE single, made the point without her needing to say it.
