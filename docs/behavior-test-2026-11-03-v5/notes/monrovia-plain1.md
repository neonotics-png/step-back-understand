# Mon Rovîa and *Bloodline*

*Researched 2026-11-03 from web sources (listed at the bottom). Where sources disagree, the note says so.*

## Who he is

- **Mon Rovîa** is the stage name of **Janjay Lowe**, a singer-songwriter born in **Monrovia, Liberia**. The name comes from his birthplace. He now lives in **Chattanooga, Tennessee**.
- He was born during Liberia's civil war. He never knew his father, and his mother died when he was a baby. American Christian missionaries took him in and adopted him. Most sources put him at **about age 7** when he was adopted, during the Second Liberian Civil War (1999–2003). One source says about 5. No Depression says the adoption kept him from becoming a child soldier.
- He grew up moving between **Florida, Montana, the Bahamas and Tennessee**. Before he got his first ukulele in Knoxville, the only music he knew was church music. He first heard folk music in Chattanooga.
- He calls his sound **"Afro-Appalachian" folk**: mostly acoustic (ukulele, guitar, fiddle), quiet and atmospheric, with lyrics about protest, resilience and identity. NPR's angle is that Appalachian folk grew partly out of West African instruments and rhythms, the banjo among them, so his music "comes full circle." The Guardian (as quoted in the label's press release) says listeners compare him to **Nick Drake and Labi Siffre**.

## *Bloodline* (2026): the album

**Basics**
- **What it is:** his first full-length album, released **January 9, 2026** on Nettwerk. It has 16 tracks and came out on CD and red vinyl. One retailer calls it his "sophomore" album, probably counting *Dark Continent*, but nearly every source, the label included, calls it his debut.
- **Producers:** Andre Samuel and his production partner Daylight. According to a Native Instruments interview, he sang full takes and they kept editing to a minimum so the songs stayed intact.
- **Singles sent ahead of release:** "Oh Wide World," "Running Boy," "Heavy Foot," "Whose Face Am I," "Field Song" and "Old Fort Steele Trail."
- **Charts:** "Heavy Foot" reached **No. 3 on Billboard's Adult Alternative Airplay chart** and No. 30 on Rock & Alternative Airplay. "Field Song" reached No. 17 on Adult Alternative Airplay. The label says the album charted on Billboard, but I couldn't confirm which chart or what position.

**What it's about**

It's a concept album that tells his life in order, from war-torn Liberia, through adoption and a childhood spent moving around the US, to adulthood. It covers survivor's guilt, not knowing his birth parents, the South's unfinished history, rest and healing, and finally peace. Pitchfork notes how he sets **calm guitar and fiddle against graphic lyrics**, placing pastoral sounds next to memories of trauma. In short, it's gentle music carrying heavy stories.

**Track by track** (summarized from his own notes in FLOOD Magazine)

The album roughly moves in four stages:

*Liberia and the war*
1. **Black Cauldron**: his origin story, which he calls "not a clean origin story, but a felt one." His life was shaped before he had any say in it.
2. **Pray the Devil Back to Hell**: honors the Liberian women who joined together during the civil war to demand peace. The title is the same as the 2008 documentary about that movement, which Leymah Gbowee led.
3. **A Day at the Soccer Fields**: written after he went back to Liberia as an adult and saw children shaped by violence. "Survival is not only about making it out alive."
4. **Bloodline**: ancestry as something still alive in him. Being far from where he was born didn't cut him off from his roots.

*Childhood and being uprooted*
5. **A Foreshadowing**: something from his childhood that he only understood years later.
6. **Little by Little**: grief and healing happen slowly.
7. **Old Fort Steele Trail**: a road in Montana he remembers for rivers, freedom and questions nobody asked out loud. It's about letting the past rest.
8. **Whose Face Am I**: not knowing his birth parents, framed as longing rather than confrontation. "Wanting to know your story is not a flaw."
9. **Running Boy**: using constant motion to avoid sitting still. "Rest is not giving up."

*Adulthood, America, the South*
10. **Field Song (To My Lover)**: grew out of melodies he hummed during long days working as a landscaper. It's for people working toward a future they can't see yet.
11. **Somewhere Down in Georgia**: Southern history that has never been reckoned with, and how a past nobody names still shapes the present.
12. **Oh Wide World**: pushes back on fear that makes staying put feel safe. "You do not need a perfect plan to begin."
13. **Code of Many Colors**: written in a stretch of doubt, when he decided to trust his own voice.
14. **Heavy Foot**: a protest song against systems that divide and distract people, calling for community and shared responsibility. This was the biggest single.

*Arriving*
15. **Infinite Pines**: finding growth in past struggles. "Fear and doubt do not disqualify you from hope."
16. **Where the Mountain Meets the Sea**: ends on peace rather than answers. "Strength does not have to mean hardness."

**Reception** (quotes mostly taken from the label's press release)
- **Pitchfork:** "approaches his complex upbringing with an impressive clarity of vision." I couldn't find the numeric score.
- **NME** (cover story): "synthesising his identity and journey into purposeful, resonant folk storytelling."
- **Billboard:** "an anthem of strength and resistance."
- **NPR:** his Afro-Appalachian sound "comes full circle."
- About 100,000 people pre-saved it before release. Belgian broadcaster VRT featured it as a culture pick.

## How to think about it

Treat *Bloodline* as an audio memoir. The EPs before it told a symbolic "hero's journey"; this album tells his own story directly. He uses folk forms that go back partly to West Africa to tell a West African story inside Appalachian music. The protest songs ("Heavy Foot," "Somewhere Down in Georgia," "Pray the Devil Back to Hell") stay quiet and hopeful rather than angry. A good way into it is to play it start to finish in order, then listen to "Heavy Foot," "Whose Face Am I," "Field Song" and "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" on their own.

## Gaps and caveats

- His age at adoption is given as 5 or 7 depending on the source. No source gives his birth year.
- The Guardian and Pitchfork quotes came through press releases. I didn't read the original reviews.
- I couldn't find the album's Billboard album-chart position or the Pitchfork score.
