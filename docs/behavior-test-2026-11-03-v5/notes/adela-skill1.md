# ADÉLA, *PRIMA* (2026)

*PRIMA* is the debut album by ADÉLA (Adéla Jergová, 22, from Bratislava, Slovakia). Capitol released it on 4 September 2026. It runs 11 tracks in 31 minutes and is a record about wanting American pop stardom, made by a girl who grew up outside America looking in. It tells that story in the sound of the American pop she grew up absorbing. Most of the album follows from three facts: where she is from, the ballet training she came out of, and the reality show that cut her early.

(I haven't heard the album. What follows comes from the credits, her interviews and the reviews.)

## What it sounds like, and why

**Glossy chart pop with a distorted bass layer.** Blake Slatkin and Dylan Brady (of 100 gecs) are co-executive producers, and between them they co-produced every track. You can hear the split between them. Slatkin is a hitmaker: "Stay," "Unholy," "About Damn Time" and "Mood." He also executive-produced the debuts of Gracie Abrams and Omar Apollo, and on *PRIMA* he co-wrote ten of the eleven songs and engineered all of them. The topline writers come from the same mainstream world: Julia Michaels, Billy Walsh and Ali Tamposi. The first five tracks were mixed by Serban Ghenea, the standard mixer for Top 40 pop. Brady comes from hyperpop, which is maximal, distorted and genre-smashing. On this album his credits are mostly synths, bass and drum programming, so his part is texture and low end, not structure. That explains how the reviews describe it. They call it "sultry, bass-focused" and say it moves *away* from the "hyperpop-heavy" sound of her 2025 EP, *The Provocateur*. Brady's style comes through openly only in places like the 1:50 "Marijuana," which Rolling Stone heard as hyperpop. Expect hooks first and noise as decoration.

**It sounds like 2000s and early-2010s pop on purpose.** The albums she says rewired her as a kid are Miley Cyrus's *Bangerz* (2013), Ariana Grande's *My Everything* (2014) and Beyoncé's self-titled album (2013). The credits point back to those eras. Theron Thomas, co-writer of Miley's "We Can't Stop," raps the refrain on "Ain't in LA." Uffie, a 2000s French electroclash figure, co-wrote "Nicole Kidman." The Dare, a revivalist of 2000s indie-sleaze, co-produced the opener "KGB." Critics heard what the credits show. The Guardian called it "a distinctive spin on the best of 00s hedonism," Clash compared "Boys" to Missy Elliott and the Neptunes, Rolling Stone heard Britney's playbook on "Boys," and The Fader heard early A$AP Rocky and *Bangerz* in "Ain't in LA." She has never said "this album is a 2013 throwback." That reading comes from me, based on the credits and the albums she names.

## What it's about, and why

**America as an imported dream.** She taught herself English from Disney Channel and American TV, and was fluent by about nine. Her mother grew up in communist Czechoslovakia, where Western media was banned, and ADÉLA's grandfather smuggled American pop posters home from his work in West German mines. Two songs come straight out of that history:
- **"KGB,"** the opener and lead single, uses the Soviet secret police as a metaphor. She is the spy who studied American pop culture from the outside and trained relentlessly to break in.
- **"Ain't in LA,"** the closer, answers it. The verse is about her mother's dream, and the video was shot in Bratislava but filled with Hollywood imagery. Its message, in her words: "You don't have to leave your country."

The album goes from wanting in to deciding you don't have to move there.

**"Nicole Kidman"** fits the same theme: a Hollywood icon used as a fantasy self. She wrote it after feeling like Kidman in a tight corset at a Golden Globes party. The video recreates *Eyes Wide Shut*, *Moulin Rouge*, *Birth*, *Babygirl* and Kidman's AMC Theatres ad. Kidman then publicly embraced the song.

**The title and the discipline come from ballet.** She trained in ballet from age three, up to eight hours a day. "PRIMA" means "first," since this is her debut, and also prima ballerina: "I'm trying to be the prima ballerina of pop." That background explains the album's tone of drive and self-assertion, and her stage show, where she dances in pointe shoes with barre and contortion moves played as "campy, wink-wink."

**The bluntness is both a persona and a reply.** She calls her music "cheeky... uncomfortably honest" and ties that to being Eastern European ("my mom is the most blunt person that I know"). She was cut in the first round of HYBE and Geffen's *Dream Academy*, the show that formed KATSEYE, and Netflix's *Pop Star Academy* documentary gave her a villain edit. She calls that year the worst of her life. Read against that, the swagger on "I'm the Man," "Red Bottoms" and "Boys" works as a rebuttal. One knowing nod: "Boys" includes uncredited spoken bits from KATSEYE's Lara Raj.

**The softer side** is deliberate too. She says she wanted to mix "the hard with the soft," and the album should "sound exactly like a 22-year-old girl." "Hitachi" is her first full ballad. "Therapy," about missing her sister, was recorded while she was sick and could barely sing, so she half-talks it over sparse piano and heavy Auto-Tune. Sources disagree on whether the sister is older or younger.

## Reception

- **Charts:** It debuted at No. 4 on the Billboard 200 with 56,000 units (31,000 of them pure sales), reached No. 2 in the UK and No. 1 in Slovakia, the Netherlands, Flanders and Scotland. "Ain't in LA" was her first Hot 100 entry, peaking at 61. "Nicole Kidman" became her first top-10 hit, both on the Hot 100 (exact peak reported inconsistently) and in the UK, where it reached No. 4.
- **Critics** liked it but were split. Metacritic is about 69. Clash gave it 8/10 and the Sydney Morning Herald 5/5. Pitchfork (5.7) and The Line of Best Fit (6/10) were cooler.
- **The recurring complaint follows from how it was built.** A record assembled from admired eras "wears its influences too heavily." Rolling Stone said "KGB" "tries too hard," and that "Starving Artist" sounds too much like Tate McRae. Critics also said she's best when she stops *announcing* stardom and just shows it. Most picked "Nicole Kidman," "Hitachi" and "Ain't in LA" as the songs most her own.

## What's hers alone

The producers and the eras explain the sound. They don't explain the point of view: an Eastern European outsider who treats American pop as a forbidden import, studies it like an agent, then decides she doesn't have to move to LA to have it. That theme, plus the ballet-trained self-discipline played as camp, is what makes *PRIMA* hers rather than a Slatkin/Brady production with a guest vocalist.
