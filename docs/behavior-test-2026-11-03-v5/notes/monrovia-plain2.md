# Mon Rovîa and *Bloodline*

## Who he is

Mon Rovîa (pronounced "Monrovia") is the stage name of **Janjay Lowe**, a Liberian-born singer-songwriter based in Chattanooga, Tennessee. He calls his music "Afro-Appalachian" folk: mostly ukulele and acoustic guitar, with fiddle and choir on some songs. It draws on Appalachian and American folk as well as West African roots.

- **Name:** He took the name from his birthplace, Monrovia, the capital of Liberia. He first used it in 2017, when he uploaded his first song to SoundCloud.
- **Childhood:** He was born during Liberia's civil wars. He never knew his father and lost his mother as an infant. Sources disagree on which war he was born during and how old he was when he was adopted (about 5 or about 7). Most accounts say American Christian missionaries took him in when he was about seven. Several profiles say this saved him from being drafted as a child soldier.
- **Growing up:** After he got refugee status, his adoptive family lived in Florida, Montana, the Bahamas and Tennessee. Until he was a teenager he heard almost nothing but religious music. He got a ukulele in Knoxville and first heard folk music in Chattanooga. He later moved to Chattanooga for college and stayed. He has said Mumford & Sons was an early comfort.
- **Identity:** He grew up as a Black immigrant child in a white, religious household, and for years he struggled to reconcile his two identities. Interviews keep returning to survivor's guilt: much of his birth family stayed in Liberia while he got out (the Guardian headline quotes him: "Why was it me?"). He has made return trips to Liberia.

## How he got here

| Year | Release / milestone |
|---|---|
| 2017 | First SoundCloud upload as Mon Rovîa |
| 2020 | *Sunburnt* EP |
| 2021 | *Dark Continent*, a mix of hip-hop, trap, R&B and folk (sources disagree on whether it was an EP or an album) |
| 2022 | Turned firmly to folk ("Vulture Culture," "Where the Rivers Run," "Borderline") |
| 2023–2025 | Four EPs built on the "hero's journey" structure: *Act 1: The Wandering*, *Act 2: Trials* (2023), *Act 3: The Dying of Self* (2024), *Act 4: Atonement* (2025) |
| 2024 | TikTok breakout ("Crooked the Road," "To Watch the World Spin Without You"; 500M+ views by Oct 2024); named a Spotify "Juniper" artist to watch; Bonnaroo debut |
| 2025 | Protest single "Heavy Foot" reached #3 on Billboard's Adult Alternative Airplay chart; Grand Ole Opry debut (July); named outstanding artist of the year for 2025 by a Liberian music awards |
| Jan 9, 2026 | Debut full-length *Bloodline* (Nettwerk) |
| 2026 | "Field Song" reached #17 on Adult Alternative Airplay; NPR Tiny Desk concert; UK and Europe tour |

He has also played Austin City Limits, Newport Folk, Boston Calling and Red Rocks. He sold out a North American headline tour (*A Place To Gather*) and opened for Alabama Shakes on three dates. His label's materials claim more than 200M lifetime streams and more than 1B TikTok views (these are promo figures).

## *Bloodline*: the album

- **What it is:** A 16-song concept album that traces his life in roughly chronological order. It starts in Monrovia during the war and moves through the places he grew up in the US to where he is now. Its themes are ancestry, migration, war and peace, survivor's guilt, grief, faith and acceptance.
- **The title:** He says ancestors "live in you and your choices and how you give yourself to the world." The point is that leaving his birthplace didn't cut him off from his roots. He says the title song "deals with the tangle of where and who I am from."
- **Sound:** The quieter tracks are just ukulele or solo guitar and voice. The bigger songs ("Heavy Foot," "Field Song") have a stomp-and-holler, sing-along feel that sits close to modern Americana and country. Pitchfork noted the contrast between soothing guitar and fiddle and graphic lyrics about war.
- **Reception:** Reviews were strong. Pitchfork praised his "impressive clarity of vision" about a complex upbringing. NPR said the sound "comes full circle, doubly honoring his identity." NME put him on its cover. The Guardian drew comparisons to Nick Drake and Labi Siffre. The album also entered a Billboard chart, though I couldn't confirm which chart or what position.

### Track by track (from his own notes in FLOOD Magazine)

1. **Black Cauldron**: An origin story that is "felt rather than tidy," about a life shaped before he could choose anything.
2. **Pray the Devil Back to Hell**: A tribute to the Liberian women who united and forced the warring sides toward peace. The title is shared with the 2008 documentary about that movement.
3. **A Day at the Soccer Fields**: Written after he went back to Liberia as an adult and saw children shaped by violence. It's about carrying what you've seen.
4. **Bloodline**: Ancestry as something still alive in him, sung with a choir.
5. **A Foreshadowing**: A childhood moment he only understood years later.
6. **Little by Little**: Grief and healing take time.
7. **Old Fort Steele Trail**: Montana, a landscape that gave him a freedom he didn't understand at the time.
8. **Whose Face Am I**: Questions about his biological parents, sung as longing rather than accusation.
9. **Running Boy**: For him, moving felt safer than staying still. Healing needs rest.
10. **Field Song (To My Lover)**: Written while he was doing hard landscaping work, for anyone working toward a future they can't see yet.
11. **Somewhere Down in Georgia**: The South's unaddressed history still shapes the present.
12. **Oh Wide World**: Against the idea that staying put is safer.
13. **Code of Many Colors**: Written during a period of self-doubt; about trusting his own voice.
14. **Heavy Foot**: The protest single. It criticizes systems that divide and distract people and calls for community responsibility.
15. **Infinite Pines**: Seeing growth in past struggles, and holding onto hope despite doubt.
16. **Where the Mountain Meets the Sea**: The album ends with peace rather than answers. Telling your own story is a kind of freedom.

## How to listen to it

Play it in order. The album's arc runs from war (tracks 1–3), through identity and his roots (4, 8), to the American places and work of his youth (7, 10, 11), and ends with acceptance (15–16). For a quick sample, try "Bloodline," "Whose Face Am I," "Heavy Foot" and "Field Song."

## Caveats

- Sources disagree on his age at adoption (5 or 7), on which civil war he was born during, and on whether *Dark Continent* was an EP or an album.
