# Mon Rovîa / Bloodline quiz answers (from memory, no lookups)

1. Bloodline is gentle, mostly acoustic indie-folk with his ukulele at the center. Guitar, banjo, mandolin, dobro, fiddle and strings support it, with piano, choirs and backing vocals behind them, and his voice is soft and high. Critics (Pitchfork, The Guardian) point out that this calm, soothing sound is set against graphic lyrics about war, violence and survival. "A Day at the Soccer Fields" is the clearest case: over a bed of sliding strings he remembers a weapon being pointed at him as a child in Liberia. ("Running Boy" does the same thing, putting a police encounter and survivor's guilt inside a sing-along chorus.)

2. It begins in Liberia, with the early tracks ("Black Cauldron," "A Day at the Soccer Fields") going back to his childhood during the civil war. The middle covers displacement, adoption into a white missionary family that moved around a lot, his identity as an adoptee ("Whose Face Am I"), and life as a Black man in the American South ("Running Boy," "Somewhere Down in Georgia"). It ends on hope and a hard-won sense of belonging: "Infinite Pines" looks toward a coming peace, and "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea" is a closing spoken epilogue. Reviews describe the arc as moving from reflection toward reconciliation. I'm working from reviews, not a full listen, so I can't map every track.

3. "Whose Face Am I" opens with spoken word in a West African (Liberian) English voice before the song starts. It's about children in conflict who lose their families, and it's framed as an adoptee's search for lineage, so hearing a Liberian voice brings the homeland and the lost family into the song directly instead of just describing them. I don't know whose voice it is or exactly what's said. Similar devices: the title track "Bloodline" is built on found sound from a church service, and the album closes with the spoken epilogue "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea."

4. He uses the term because Appalachian music already has African roots. The banjo descends from West African gourd lutes, and mountain music formed when African, Irish and Scottish traditions mixed (he talks about enslaved Africans escaping into the mountains). It matters to him because he grew up seeing folk as a "white experience" and didn't think he could make it. Learning that the banjo came from West Africa, where he's from, let him claim the genre as partly his inheritance. He also says Black contributors to that tradition got "left in the dust," and he wants young Black listeners to feel free to tell their stories through folk.

5. As a teenager he listened to Kendrick Lamar and Lil Baby. His early work, from 2017 SoundCloud uploads through the 2020 Sunburnt EP and the 2021 Dark Continent, mixed "sing-song rap," trap, R&B and folk, partly because he saw almost no Black artists in indie-folk and thought folk wasn't open to him. Personally, he had loved singer-songwriters, and specifically the Fleet Foxes, Bon Iver and Radical Face records his foster brothers played for him. His parents gave him a ukulele, and during COVID he decided to "go back to what I love, just writing on the ukulele," feeling the moment was "ripe for singer-songwriters" and that people were ready to hear about healing. On the career side, around 2022 he moved to folk songs that took off on TikTok, then built an audience with the four "Act" EPs before Bloodline. His voice also suited folk better: people called it "unique," not polished like pop or hip-hop.

6. Liberian: "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" shares its title with a 2008 documentary about the Liberian women's peace movement that helped end the second civil war. I didn't confirm he intended that reference, though it fits his civil-war material. "A Day at the Soccer Fields" and "Black Cauldron" also go back to Liberia's war. American: the Appalachian string-band and banjo tradition, heard in "Old Fort Steele Trail" (banjo over a steady drum) and "Field Song" (claps, stomps, mandolin and dobro, which suggests a work or chain-gang song). "Code of Many Colors" also seems to echo Dolly Parton's East Tennessee "Coat of Many Colors," which is again unconfirmed. Gospel and church music, from his upbringing as a pastor's son, run through the title track.

7. The praise was strong. Pitchfork cited his "impressive clarity of vision," NME called it "purposeful, resonant folk storytelling," NPR said his sound "comes full circle," The Guardian praised its "arresting serenity" and compared him to Nick Drake and Labi Siffre, and it entered the Billboard charts. Reservation one: Pitchfork found the clash between the soothing sound and graphic lyrics on "Running Boy" "outright uncomfortable," which shows the album chooses to deliver trauma in a soft, palatable sound rather than a harsh one, and some listeners may find that jarring. Reservation two: At The Barrier called the closing spoken epilogue somewhat superfluous and said they'd skip it, which shows how committed the album is to telling a story in order, even when that costs it some musical momentum. A third, smaller point: the songs are short vignettes (16 songs, about 43 minutes), which some call a gift but which means individual songs don't develop for long.

8. The album isn't only about Liberia. It traces a whole life, including growing up Black in Tennessee, and it looks at the legacy of violence, racism and oppression in both Liberia and the United States. "Heavy Foot" (government lies and oppression, with community and solidarity as the answer) and "Somewhere Down in Georgia" (racism in the American South) are the US side of the same story, not a detour. He studies Civil Rights history because he sees parallels with today, and he credits Baldwin, Morrison and Baraka as the writers who gave him confidence. He would likely present them as protest and resilience songs that continue the album's themes, with both his "bloodlines," Liberian and American, carrying the same history of struggle. I didn't find a direct quote from him introducing these two songs, so that framing is inferred from his interviews.
