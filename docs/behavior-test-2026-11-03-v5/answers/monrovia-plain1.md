# Mon Rovîa / *Bloodline*: quiz answers (from memory, no lookups)

**1. Sound and tension.**
The album is mostly acoustic and atmospheric. Sources mention ukulele, which is his signature instrument, along with guitar and fiddle. Coverage described "warm acoustic textures and atmospheric arrangements." I don't know for sure which instrument leads on each track. Pitchfork (in a summary I read) heard soothing guitar and fiddle under graphic lyrics, with pastoral sounds set against memories of trauma. "A Day at the Soccer Fields" is one example: he wrote it after going back to Liberia and seeing children shaped by violence, and the gentle folk setting carries that subject.

**2. Sequencing as memoir.**
It opens in Liberia and the war. "Black Cauldron" is his origin story, then come "Pray the Devil Back to Hell," "A Day at the Soccer Fields" and the title track about ancestry. The middle covers childhood and being uprooted in America: "A Foreshadowing," "Old Fort Steele Trail" (a Montana road), "Whose Face Am I" (not knowing his birth parents) and "Running Boy." After that it moves to adult life and America, with "Field Song" from his landscaping days, "Somewhere Down in Georgia," "Oh Wide World," "Code of Many Colors" and "Heavy Foot." It ends with "Infinite Pines" and "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea," which settle on peace rather than answers.

**3. Spoken voices / found audio.**
I don't know. Nothing I read said whether "Whose Face Am I" or any other track uses spoken word or found audio. What I do know is that "Whose Face Am I" is about not knowing his biological parents, framed as longing rather than confrontation ("wanting to know your story is not a flaw"). I can't name another track that uses such a device without guessing.

**4. "Afro-Appalachian."**
The term points to Appalachian folk's partial roots in West African instruments and rhythms, the banjo's lineage being the best-known example. NPR's line was that his sound "comes full circle." For a Liberian-born Black artist who first met folk music in Chattanooga, the label lets him claim the genre as partly his own heritage rather than borrowed ground. It connects his two identities, African and American, which coverage says he wrestled with for years. I didn't read a direct quote from him explaining the term, so the personal motive is my inference from coverage.

**5. Shift from hip-hop-blended work to folk.**
The timeline is clear. The *Sunburnt* EP came out in 2020, and *Dark Continent* in 2021 mixed hip-hop, trap, R&B and folk. In 2022 he turned toward folk ("Vulture Culture," "Where the Rivers Run," "Borderline"), followed by the four "Act" EPs from 2023 to 2025 and *Bloodline*. No Depression mentioned a "more precise approach to songwriting." I don't know his stated career or personal reasons for the change. My best guess is that folk storytelling fit the autobiographical and protest material better, and that TikTok traction on his folk songs helped. Both points are inference, not something I sourced.

**6. Traditions drawn on.**
Liberian: "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" honors the Liberian women who united during the civil war to demand peace. That movement was led by Leymah Gbowee, and the 2008 documentary has the same title. American: the Appalachian folk tradition shapes the album's sound, and "Somewhere Down in Georgia" takes on Southern history that has never been fully addressed. "Old Fort Steele Trail" adds American landscape to the Western and pastoral side. "Heavy Foot" also sits in the American protest-folk line, calling for community against systems that divide and distract.

**7. Critical reception.**
The praise was strong. Pitchfork wrote of "an impressive clarity of vision," NME called it "purposeful, resonant folk storytelling," Billboard heard "an anthem of strength and resistance," the Guardian noted comparisons to Nick Drake and Labi Siffre, and NPR said the sound "comes full circle." I don't know of any specific critical reservations. Everything I read came through label press materials or summaries, which highlight praise, so I can't name two reservations without inventing them.

**8. Misreading "Somewhere Down in Georgia" and "Heavy Foot" as a departure.**
The album isn't only about Liberia. It's a memoir of his whole path, Liberia to adoption to growing up and working in the US South, so his American life is part of the bloodline story rather than a detour from it. "Somewhere Down in Georgia" is about how an unnamed past still shapes the present, which is the same logic the title track applies to his own ancestry. "Heavy Foot" calls for community and shared responsibility, echoing the Liberian women's peace movement earlier on the record. He would likely present both as continuations: lessons about history, survival and unity that he carries from Liberia into his American life. That last point is inferred from his track notes, not a direct quote.
