# ADÉLA – PRIMA quiz answers (from research memory, no new lookups)

**1. "KGB" persona**
She casts herself as a spy from the former Eastern Bloc quietly "infiltrating" the American music industry. The point is that she is an outsider, a Slovak kid who learned English from American TV and was cut from *Dream Academy*, working her way in on her own plan. One reviewer also read the spy idea as her working quietly until she was ready to go public. She handles the Soviet imagery with dark, deadpan humour about her country's history and her upbringing, using it as a joke, a threat and a badge of Eastern European identity. She also frames the song as a reply to online critics who once mocked her. I saw one source call the KGB reference controversial, but I didn't find a detailed statement from her defending it.

**2. "Ain't In LA"**
The song comes from a photo of her mother at 18 posing in front of Western pop posters (Madonna, Marilyn Monroe, Keanu Reeves) that her grandfather had smuggled in from Germany under communism. Her mother once dreamed of moving to LA but got pregnant instead. Its message is that talent, style and beauty don't belong only to Los Angeles. She wrote it for her mother "and the billion other bitches who are probably cooler than me," meaning girls in overlooked cities. She has added that "LA is what it is because of all the immigrants, just like myself." As the closer, it turns the album's ambition back toward home: the dream of conquering LA becomes a celebration of Bratislava and of the women who never got there. The video, shot in Bratislava, features her mother and sister.

**3. "The hard with the soft"**
She meant blending vulnerability with swagger, and muscular beats with feminine energy. The record should sound like a 22-year-old girl, who is both cocky and insecure. Upbeat tracks like "Boys" (a 2000s club stomper about men who hover around women at clubs) or "I'm The Man" (bass-heavy, about being the boss in a relationship) carry the hard, playful side. "Therapy" is the soft extreme: barely sung, spoken and heavily Auto-Tuned because her voice was gone. In it she talks about her father, guilt over missing her little sister growing up, and the cost of moving away. Ballads like "Hitachi" and "Red Bottoms" sit in between, pairing jokes or glamour with insecurity.

**4. Title and mainstream aim**
"Prima" means "first," since it is her debut, and refers to the prima ballerina, the top dancer in a company. She trained as a professional ballerina from childhood and says she wants to be "the prima ballerina of pop." The polished sound fits that aim: she calls herself a student of pop and wanted the album to draw on the pop she loved as a preteen, around 2014. She has said she wanted to sing more. She also said the EP was driven by anger over the TV show and "wasn't truly about me as a person," while Blake Slatkin encouraged her to stop second-guessing outside expectations. I didn't find her explicitly saying "I chose mainstream over weird." That reading comes mostly from critics and fans, so treat it as interpretation.

**5. Frankness about sex and the body**
She describes herself as blunt, dry and sarcastic. She calls her music "cheeky" and "uncomfortably honest," and links that bluntness to being an Eastern European woman ("a little feisty") and to her very direct mother. She uses humour to approach heavy or intimate subjects. "Hitachi" began as a silly joke song about a vibrator, then became "a realer song" about intimacy fading in a relationship, with "Fantasize" as its follow-up: communicate or leave. "I'm the Man" comes from her real dating frustration at out-earning men her age and wanting a partner secure enough to let her lead. Artistically, the label framed the album's themes as femininity and bodily autonomy, so owning these topics is part of the point. I don't know much more about her reasoning than this.

**6. Path to PRIMA**
- **Ballet:** She trained in Bratislava, at the Vienna State Ballet and at the English National Ballet School until COVID sent her home, where she turned to music.
- **Dream Academy:** She joined HYBE x Geffen's *Dream Academy*, shown as Netflix's *Pop Star Academy: KATSEYE*. She was eliminated early after a fan vote and later said contestants "had no control over our own image."
- **Starting over in LA:** She stayed in LA, got her GED while living in her vocal coach's attic, and released "Homewrecked" and "Superscar" (2024), then the Grimes-co-produced "MachineGirl." She signed with Capitol in May 2025.
- **The Provocateur:** Her EP (August 2025) leaned into a gritty, provocative, polarizing persona; she has said "I've always pushed people's buttons."
- **How PRIMA relates:** It keeps her confidence and some of the same producers (Dylan Brady, Blake Slatkin). It swaps the anger and edge for more personal, polished, nostalgic pop, and many fans and critics saw it as tamer. "KGB" works as the bridge between the two eras.

**7. Critical reception**
Reviews were favourable but mixed, around a reported Metacritic 68 that I couldn't verify directly. Clash gave 8/10 and Rolling Stone said her risk-taking paid off. The Line of Best Fit gave 6/10, Sputnik 3.0, and Ringtone called it "music to shop to." Admirers praised her wit, personality and star quality. Skeptics said it traded the EP's strangeness for formulaic, reference-heavy pop that wears its influences on its sleeve (Britney, Ariana, Gwen Stefani), with too many slow songs. Nearly everyone agreed "Ain't In LA" is the highlight. "Nicole Kidman" and "Red Bottoms" were also widely liked.

**8. The "revenge record" expectation**
They would find almost nothing aimed at KATSEYE. The album is about ambition, home, family, sex and relationships. KATSEYE's Lara Raj actually appears on "Boys," and KATSEYE members came to her LA tour show. She did the anger-processing on the earlier EP. Since then she has called the elimination a blessing in disguise, said she knew she wasn't right for that project, and joked the cast is "forever trauma-bonded." The nearest echo of the show is "KGB," which turns her outsider and underdog story into a confident spy myth rather than a grudge. I didn't research the specific "villain edit" claims in depth, so I can't speak to how she has addressed that label directly.
