# Mon Rovîa and *Bloodline*: Expert Comprehension Quiz

Answer each question in 2-4 sentences.

1. Describe the core sound of *Bloodline* (lead instrument, supporting instrumentation, overall texture) and explain the main tension critics hear between how the music sounds and what the lyrics say. Use one specific song to show that tension at work.

2. *Bloodline* is often described as a memoir told across 16 songs. Explain how the album's sequencing carries a life story: roughly where it begins, what ground it covers in the middle, and how it ends.

3. Several moments on *Bloodline* use spoken voices or found audio rather than only sung lyrics. Explain how "Whose Face Am I" uses this device and what it contributes to the album's search for origins, citing at least one other place on the record where a similar device appears.

4. Why does Mon Rovîa call his music "Afro-Appalachian," and why does that framing matter to him personally as a Black, Liberian-born artist working in folk?

5. His early recordings did not sound like *Bloodline*. Explain the career and personal reasons behind his move from rap-leaning, hip-hop-blended releases to the intimate, ukulele-led folk of the album.

6. Name two musical or historical traditions *Bloodline* draws on or answers, and show how a specific song connects to each. Your answer should cover at least one Liberian and one American reference point.

7. Summarize how critics received *Bloodline*, including the main praise and at least two specific reservations, and explain what each reservation says about the album's approach.

8. A radio host plans to introduce "Somewhere Down in Georgia" and "Heavy Foot" as a "departure" from an album that is otherwise about Mon Rovîa's Liberian roots. Using what you know about the artist and the album, explain why that framing misreads the record and how Mon Rovîa himself would likely frame those two songs.
