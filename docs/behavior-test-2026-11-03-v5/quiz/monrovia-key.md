## 1. Sound and the sound/lyric tension

**Model answer:** *Bloodline* is a mostly acoustic, modern folk record built around Mon Rovîa's ukulele (his main instrument) and his soft, clear, high voice, with acoustic guitar, banjo and mandolin up front and strings, piano, dobro, marimba, sparing choirs and synths adding texture. Critics hear a deliberate "sweet-meets-bitter" tension: gentle, pastoral and often upbeat music carries lyrics about war, trauma, displacement and political anger. "Heavy Foot" shows it clearly: it is a bright, sing-along song that moves from choral chant into bluegrass, yet it is a "deceptively uptempo indictment" of political leadership, which he calls "a song about joy and resistance."

**Rubric:**
- 2: Identifies ukulele-led acoustic folk with at least two other supporting instruments or textures, AND explains the gentle-sound/dark-lyric tension with a correct song example (e.g., "Heavy Foot," or "Black Cauldron" pairing warm textures with violent childhood memory).
- 1: Gets the acoustic folk sound right but misses or garbles the tension, OR describes the tension with no accurate song example.
- 0: Describes the album as primarily electronic, hip-hop or rock, or gives no real description.

## 2. How the sequencing tells a life story

**Model answer:** The album opens in Liberia, with early songs ("Black Cauldron," "Pray the Devil Back to Hell," "A Day at the Soccer Fields," the title track) about fragmentary childhood memory, civil war and inherited lineage. The middle follows displacement and life in America: the search for unknown origins ("Whose Face Am I"), Montana memories ("Old Fort Steele Trail"), manual labor ("Field Song"), the South's violent history ("Somewhere Down in Georgia") and open protest ("Heavy Foot"). It closes gently with the spoken-word "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea," which offers peace rather than answers, so the arc runs from reflection and grief toward reconciliation.

**Rubric:**
- 2: Correctly places the start in Liberia/childhood and war, describes a middle that moves through displacement, identity and American life (labor, history or protest), and an ending that turns toward peace, gratitude or reconciliation.
- 1: Gets the general "Liberia to America" movement but no sense of the ending, or misplaces major songs.
- 0: No coherent arc, or claims the album is unsequenced or chronologically reversed.

## 3. Spoken voices and found audio ("Whose Face Am I")

**Model answer:** "Whose Face Am I" opens with spoken word from his sister, whom he reconnected with as an adult; she speaks in Liberian English (Kolokwa), calls him by his childhood name and talks about his biological father, and reviewers describe him piecing together his origin story from these voicemail-like fragments. The device makes the search for roots literal: the identity he is trying to recover arrives as incomplete pieces in the actual voice of his bloodline, which fits a song about the ache of not knowing your parents. Similar documentary moments recur: excerpts of their conversations act as interludes, "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" opens with a news excerpt hinting at why he left Liberia, the title track opens with found church audio, and the album ends on a spoken-word epilogue.

**Rubric:**
- 2: Identifies the sister's spoken voice (or voicemail/conversation fragments in Liberian English) on "Whose Face Am I," explains how it embodies piecing together a fragmented origin, AND names one other correct instance (news clip, church audio, sister interludes or spoken closer).
- 1: Identifies the spoken intro but gives no interpretation, or interprets well but cites no correct second instance.
- 0: Wrong source of the voice (e.g., his adoptive parents, a sample from another artist) with no valid interpretation.

## 4. Why "Afro-Appalachian"

**Model answer:** He is African-born and lives in Chattanooga in the foothills of Appalachia, and learning that the banjo descends from West Africa, "right there on that coast" where he is from, "really fortified" him; he also points to the Black roots of the region's string music, where enslaved Africans met Irish and Scottish settlers and their instruments blended, though their songs "got left in the dust." The label matters because he once saw folk as "a white experience" and "hadn't seen many people... that looked like me playing folk music," so it stakes his claim to the genre as an inheritance rather than a borrowing. It also lets him reclaim an under-documented African American folk history and show young Black listeners they "can do something different" and still tell their story.

**Rubric:**
- 2: Gives both the factual basis (African birth plus Appalachian home, banjo's West African roots or Black roots of Appalachian music) AND the personal stakes (overcoming the sense that folk was white, claiming belonging, reclaiming erased history, representation).
- 1: Gives only one of those two halves, or only "it mixes African and Appalachian sounds" with no reasoning.
- 0: Treats it as a marketing tag with no grounding, or misstates the history (e.g., says the banjo came from Ireland).

## 5. Why he moved from rap-leaning releases to intimate folk

**Model answer:** He began on SoundCloud making mostly rap because it looked like "the way into the music scene," and early releases such as *Sunburnt* and *Dark Continent* blended folk instruments with hip-hop, trap and R&B. Raised in a church-centered evangelical household that shielded him from secular music, he had found comfort in folk (Mumford & Sons, Bon Iver, Fleet Foxes) and in its unusual voices, and during the pandemic he took up the ukulele and chose to go "back to what I love, just writing on the ukulele," with his manager pushing him toward personal, introspective songs. That shift let him stop "running from identity": songs about adoption, war and survivor's guilt became a way to heal, which is why he calls *Bloodline* "the journey through my own becoming."

**Rubric:**
- 2: Covers the career side (rap as the apparent entry point and the hip-hop-blended early work) AND the personal/artistic side (folk as his real love, ukulele, introspection, confronting identity or survivor's guilt as healing).
- 1: Covers only one side, or says he "switched to folk" without a credible reason.
- 0: Claims he always made folk or that the change was label-imposed, with no supported reasoning.

## 6. Traditions the album draws on or answers

**Model answer:** On the Liberian side, "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" honors the Liberian women who demanded peace during the civil war; its title echoes Leymah Gbowee's interfaith women's movement, which vowed to "pray the devil back to hell" in answer to Charles Taylor, and the song has a gospel feel. On the American side, the album draws on Appalachian string-band and bluegrass music with its African roots (the banjo on "Old Fort Steele Trail," the bluegrass turn in "Heavy Foot") and on Black work songs and spirituals: "Field Song," written from his landscaping and groundskeeping labor, evokes chain-gang music but turns it joyful and is called a "modern day spiritual." "Somewhere Down in Georgia" answers the South's history of racial violence, and critics also hear Paul Simon's *Graceland*, Cat Stevens and Bon Iver in the arrangements.

**Rubric:**
- 2: Names at least one accurate Liberian reference point (the women's peace movement behind "Pray the Devil Back to Hell," or Liberian civil war history) and one accurate American tradition (Appalachian/bluegrass with African roots, spirituals or work songs, Southern history), each tied to a correct song.
- 1: Names traditions accurately but ties only one to a song, or covers only the American or only the Liberian side.
- 0: Traditions named are wrong or unconnected to the album.

## 7. Critical reception and its reservations

**Model answer:** Reception was broadly warm: Atwood called it "breathtakingly beautiful," Americana Highways a serious, emotionally direct debut with "endlessly listenable" vocals, At The Barrier "a confident and competent piece of work" that "should widen his audience," and Pitchfork praised his "impressive clarity of vision" about his upbringing. The reservations are telling. Pitchfork felt that at 16 tracks it "occasionally lapses into more generic imagery about overcoming fear" and that "Heavy Foot" needs "more substantial scaffolding" for global issues, which reflects the cost of his encouraging, companion-to-the-listener register; At The Barrier found the spoken-word epilogue "somewhat superfluous" and some vignettes slight, showing that the narrative devices serve the story more than replay; and VRT questioned the "Afro" prefix since folk already has African roots, showing the genre label is debated even by admirers.

**Rubric:**
- 2: Conveys broadly positive reception with at least one accurate source of praise, AND gives at least two accurate reservations with a sensible reading of what each reveals.
- 1: Correct overall tone plus one reservation, or two reservations with no explanation.
- 0: Claims it was panned or mixed-to-negative, or invents criticisms not in the sources.

## 8. Apply: the "departure" framing

**Model answer:** The framing misreads the album because "bloodline" means inherited history carried forward, and his history includes the American South he grew up in; reviewers note the record casts "a critical eye on his now-home while maintaining emotional ties to the place that gave him life," and he treats personal reflection and public conscience as inseparable. He would likely frame "Somewhere Down in Georgia" as the same act of naming the past that drives his personal songs: at the Tiny Desk he stressed telling history accurately so it is not repeated, and he says "the past does not disappear when it goes unnamed." He would frame "Heavy Foot" as "joy and resistance," the outward turn of a survivor's story ("If I have mine and my freedom, I want to free other people as well"; "Change does not happen alone"), and both songs keep the album's signature sound, bright and communal music carrying hard truths.

**Rubric:**
- 2: Argues the songs are continuous with the album's theme of inherited history and identity (Liberian roots and American home as one story), AND predicts his framing using supported ideas: truthful history so it is not repeated, naming the past, joy and resistance, freedom for others, community.
- 1: Says the framing is wrong but gives only a generic reason ("it's all personal"), or gets his likely framing of only one song.
- 0: Agrees the songs are a departure, or predicts a framing contradicted by his statements (e.g., that he avoids politics).
