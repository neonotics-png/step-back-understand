## 1. "KGB" and the spy persona (what it is / how it works)

**Model answer:** "KGB" casts ADÉLA as a spy or "agent provocateur" from the former Soviet sphere, quietly absorbing American pop culture and infiltrating the US entertainment industry as an Eastern European outsider. The song is really about ambition and work ethic: her childhood dreams of pop domination and the years of effort it took to break in, which she frames as determination rather than arrogance. Its video closes with a disclaimer that the Soviet imagery is an acknowledgment of where she is from, not an endorsement, and that she stands with the people of Ukraine. Sonically it is the high-energy, cheeky synth track closest to her earlier EP.

**Rubric:**
- 2 points: Identifies the spy/infiltrator persona AND links it to her outsider position (Eastern European newcomer breaking into American pop) and to ambition/hard work. Mentioning the Ukraine/"where I'm from" disclaimer or the link to the EP's sound is a strong plus but not required if the first two parts are clear.
- 1 point: Identifies the spy persona but treats it as a generic "cool" image without connecting it to her outsider status or ambition, or explains her ambition without the persona.
- 0 points: Misidentifies the song's concept (for example, says it is a political endorsement or a love song) or gives no relevant content.

## 2. How "Ain't In LA" works (what it is / how it works)

**Model answer:** The song grew from a photo of her mother at 18 posing in front of a wall of Western pop-culture posters, at a time when that culture was banned in communist Czechoslovakia; she wrote it for her mother and the many women like her "who will never get their flowers." Its message is that you do not have to leave your country to matter, because there is beauty and talent where you are from. After an album full of striving toward American stardom, it turns the lens back on home and on the people who never made it to the big city, and its video was shot in Bratislava while being packed with LA and Hollywood references. Built on 808s, glitchy drums and a pitched, chopped vocal, it was widely singled out by critics as the album's best or defining song.

**Rubric:**
- 2 points: Explains the origin in her mother's story under communism (posters/photo) AND the "you don't need to be in LA" message, and shows how it reframes the album's ambition toward home or those left behind.
- 1 point: Gets either the origin or the message but not how they fit together, or describes it only as a generic hometown-pride anthem without the mother/communism background.
- 0 points: Misreads the song (for example, as a song about loving or moving to LA) or offers nothing accurate.

## 3. "The hard with the soft" (what it is / how it works)

**Model answer:** She said she wanted PRIMA to "sound exactly like a 22-year-old girl," mixing "the hard with the soft, the vulnerable with the swag of it all, the muscular beats with the feminine energy." On the upbeat side, club tracks such as "Boys," "Fantasize," "KGB" and "I'm the Man" pair heavy bass and dance beats with racy, deadpan humor and confidence, while ballads like "Red Bottoms," "Hitachi" and "Therapy" expose insecurity and longing. "Therapy" is the clearest soft moment: she recorded it with her voice nearly gone, calls it "like a therapy session," and it deals with frustration with her father, missing her younger sister, and pressure from industry executives, which critics heard as the album's sad, homesick emotional core.

**Rubric:**
- 2 points: States her intent (sound like a 22-year-old; hard/soft, vulnerable/swagger) AND gives an accurate upbeat example AND accurately describes what "Therapy" is about (family distance and/or industry pressure, raw personal tone).
- 1 point: Describes the contrast in general terms but gets only one of the examples right, or describes "Therapy" vaguely as "a sad song" without its subject.
- 0 points: Does not grasp the swagger/vulnerability contrast or describes the songs inaccurately.

## 4. The title and the mainstream aim (why it is the way it is)

**Model answer:** "Prima" means "first," marking her debut, and also refers to the prima ballerina, the top soloist in a ballet company; she trained in ballet from early childhood and says she is trying to be "the prima ballerina of pop," with this album as her first step. That goal explains the polished, mainstream design: she has openly said she wants to be hugely popular in the mold of Britney and Gaga, believes "popular" and "cool" can coexist, and talks about playing arenas. The 2000s and 2010s pop, R&B and trap-pop sounds also reflect the American pop she absorbed growing up in Slovakia and returned to after Dream Academy, and she wanted the record to feel like her at 22 rather than a continuation of a niche style.

**Rubric:**
- 2 points: Explains both meanings of the title (debut and prima ballerina/ballet background) AND connects the mainstream sound to her stated ambition for big pop stardom and/or her roots in the pop she grew up on.
- 1 point: Explains the title correctly but not the reason for the sound, or explains the mainstream ambition without the ballet meaning of the title.
- 0 points: Wrong or missing explanation of the title and no credible reason for the sound.

## 5. Frankness and provocation (why it is the way it is)

**Model answer:** ADÉLA ties her bluntness to being an Eastern European woman and to her mother, calls her music "cheeky" and "uncomfortably honest," and says she has always enjoyed pushing people's buttons and making them uncomfortable. Growing up in what she calls a patriarchal, conservative Slovakia made openness about her body feel radical, and she has said Dream Academy wanted her to tone that side down. She frames her sexual frankness as a deliberate statement: reclaiming sexuality from a "sexy" defined by men and commenting on the overcommodification of women in pop, while arguing the backlash against the male gaze has "swung a little too far." Humor is her delivery method, so "Boys" mocks objectifying men, "I'm the Man" plays with gender expectations, and "Hitachi" uses a joke title for a song about missing intimacy.

**Rubric:**
- 2 points: Gives at least one personal reason (Slovak/Eastern European bluntness, her mother, conservative upbringing, being told to tone down) AND at least one artistic reason (enjoying discomfort, reclaiming sexuality from the male gaze, critique of how the industry sells women), with some sense that humor is the vehicle.
- 1 point: Gives only personal or only artistic reasons, or says simply that "sex sells" without her stated intent.
- 0 points: No reasons grounded in her own statements, or treats the provocation as accidental.

## 6. The path to PRIMA (context)

**Model answer:** ADÉLA was eliminated early from HYBE and Geffen's Dream Academy, the competition that formed KATSEYE, and Netflix's Pop Star Academy docuseries gave her a "villain" or mean-girl image. She answered by self-releasing singles such as "Homewrecked" and "Superscar," which caught the attention of Grimes (who co-produced "MachineGirl") and led to a major-label deal, and then the 2025 EP The Provocateur, a clubby, hyperpop and electroclash record whose provocative cover and "SexOnTheBeat" video critiqued the industry's sexualization of women and turned the villain narrative into a weapon. PRIMA keeps some of that edge (especially "KGB") but largely shifts toward glossier 2000s and 2010s-style pop, R&B-trap and ballads, which some saw as maturation and others as a step back from the EP's daring.

**Rubric:**
- 2 points: Covers the Dream Academy/KATSEYE elimination and villain image, the independent singles/EP phase with its provocative, experimental sound, AND how PRIMA relates to it (continuity in places, but a turn toward more mainstream pop).
- 1 point: Covers the background accurately but not the EP-to-album shift, or describes the shift but not the background.
- 0 points: Major factual errors (for example, says she was in KATSEYE) or no relevant context.

## 7. Critical reception (context)

**Model answer:** Admirers such as Clash, Rolling Stone and Ratings Game Music praised her star quality, wit, ambition and range, with Rolling Stone adding that she is most convincing when she shows her confidence rather than announcing it. Skeptics such as The Line of Best Fit, Sputnik Music and Ringtone argued that she traded the left-field edge of The Provocateur for familiar templates, wearing pop history "like high fashion accessories" (Britney and Neptunes-style production on "Boys," Ariana Grande-like trap on "Nicole Kidman") and leaning on weaker mid-tempo songs and ballads, a "formulaic approach" that helps her popularity but holds back her artistry. Defenders replied that nostalgia is a pop language she uses as raw material, and nearly everyone agreed that the closer "Ain't In LA" was a high point.

**Rubric:**
- 2 points: Identifies the split as originality/identity versus commercial polish and borrowed references (relative to the EP), gives at least one concrete example of what each side valued or criticized, AND names "Ain't In LA" as the common highlight.
- 1 point: Describes reception as "mixed" with some correct detail but misses the core disagreement or the shared highlight.
- 0 points: Claims universal acclaim or universal panning, or offers no accurate reasoning.

## 8. The "villain" expecting a revenge record (apply / predict)

**Model answer:** They would not find a grudge record against KATSEYE: "Boys" even features an interlude from KATSEYE member Lara Raj, a friendly nod to her trainee past, and the most personal songs are about family, home and industry pressure ("Therapy," "Ain't In LA"). This fits how she talks about the show: she says she knew she was not right for the group, loves its members, believes "everything happens for a reason," and treats being cast as the villain as part of being a public persona "there to be consumed." Her defiance is real but is aimed at doubters and industry norms rather than the group, channeled into ambition ("KGB," wanting to be the prima ballerina of pop) and provocation; the more direct answer to the villain edit was her earlier EP, The Provocateur.

**Rubric:**
- 2 points: Predicts that the album is not an anti-KATSEYE revenge record, supports it with at least one album detail (the Lara Raj cameo on "Boys," or the focus on family/home/ambition) AND with her stated attitude toward the show (not right for it, friendly with members, villain role as part of public life).
- 1 point: Reaches the right conclusion but supports it with only album evidence or only her statements, or vaguely says "she moved on."
- 0 points: Agrees it is a revenge record against KATSEYE, or gives no reasoning.
