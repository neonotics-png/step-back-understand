# PRIMA quiz answers (from memory, no lookups)

1. "KGB" casts ADÉLA as a kind of spy or operative. One review described it as framing her entry into American pop as a spy story, and the title uses the Soviet secret police as a metaphor for the relentless, almost regimented training she put herself through to become a pop star. That fits a newcomer from Eastern Europe arriving in the US industry as an outsider who has drilled for this, the way a ballet school or the Dream Academy trainee system drills you. Beyond her saying that everything in the song is meant literally, I don't know how she discussed or handled the Soviet imagery itself (for example, any political caveats). Rolling Stone thought the song tried too hard to sell her ambition.

2. "Ain't In LA" was inspired by a photo of her mother at 18 in front of a wall of Western pop posters, which ADÉLA's grandfather had smuggled into communist Czechoslovakia from Germany. The song pictures a girl in hand-me-downs, sharing a room, with Madonna and Destiny's Child on her wall. Its message is that you don't have to be from a big city to matter: in her words, "you don't have to be from a big city to be a bad bitch," with LA standing for "something bigger." Up to that point the album's ambition is aimed at LA, so ending here turns the story back toward home and toward dreamers everywhere. She reinforced this by shooting the video in Bratislava. Theron Thomas, co-writer of "We Can't Stop," gives it a 2013-style slowed vocal intro.

3. She said she wanted the album to "sound exactly like a 22-year-old girl," mixing "the hard with the soft" and "the vulnerable with the swag of it all," which means muscular beats and bravado next to openness and fragility. The hard side shows up in tracks like "Boys," a bass-heavy, percussive club song about being fed up with male attention, and "KGB." "Therapy" is the soft extreme: a sparse piano ballad with whispery, autotuned vocals about her family, especially her father, and about being far from home. Reviewers noted the whiplash between the two, and some found "Therapy" the most affecting song on the album.

4. PRIMA means "first," since it's her debut, and it nods to the prima ballerina, the lead dancer in a company. That ties to her ballet training from age three (Slovak National Theatre, Vienna State Ballet, English National Ballet School) and to her stated goal of being "the prima ballerina of pop." She also says she learned English from American pop stars and grew up loving Beyoncé, Britney and others. She told Paper that the EP was "me very much being like, this has to be me," and that its songs were good but not "Julia Michaels level good pop songs." For the album she wanted to use "everybody's superpowers" (Slatkin, Brady, Michaels) and felt "much more at peace." She also said she doesn't want to be polarizing just to be polarizing.

5. Most of the frankness is described in reviews rather than explained by her. "Hitachi" is a breakup ballad named after a vibrator brand, "I'm the Man" plays with gender roles, and "Boys" mocks male attention. Reviewers saw this as carrying on the EP's themes of sexual liberation, delivered "not with a wink, but with unwavering eye contact." Her own stated reasons I can only partly give: she wanted the album to sound like a real 22-year-old girl, chaotic and cheeky; she wanted it to be music the *Spring Breakers* girls would listen to; and she thinks art should "start a conversation," though not through rage-bait. She also said she is "getting tired" of the provocative image and is "not cunty all the time." I don't know of any more specific personal explanation she has given.

6. After a ballet career that COVID interrupted, she joined HYBE and Geffen's Dream Academy (2023), a K-pop-style trainee competition, and was the first contestant eliminated. The winners became KATSEYE. Netflix's *Pop Star Academy: KATSEYE* then showed her in a way that led fans to call her a "mean girl" over comments about another contestant's work ethic, so her public image started out as polarizing. She released singles from 2024 ("Homewrecked," "Superscar"), got Grimes's attention and a Grimes co-production ("Machine Girl"), signed to Capitol in 2025, and released *The Provocateur* (August 2025). That EP was gritty, industrial hyperpop with a deliberately shocking cover, which leaned into the provocateur image. PRIMA keeps Dylan Brady and the confidence, but it is more polished, mainstream, song-first pop. "KGB" was seen as the bridge between the two.

7. Reception was favorable but split, with a Metacritic score in the high 60s. Variety and Clash (8/10) were near the top, and Pitchfork was reportedly the most skeptical. Admirers praised her star presence, her vocals and better-written songs. Skeptics said it was a step back from the stranger EP into safer, bass-heavy mainstream pop that wears its influences (Britney, Ariana, Addison Rae, Charli) too openly. Both sides tended to agree on "Ain't In LA" as a high point. Even a mixed Sputnikmusic review said the album ends on a high note with it. "Nicole Kidman" and "Hitachi" were also widely singled out.

8. They wouldn't find a revenge record. As far as I know, nothing on the album targets KATSEYE. The ambition songs ("KGB," "Starving Artist") are about her own drive and about the industry in general, and the emotional core is about home, family and distance ("Therapy," "Ain't In LA"). KATSEYE's Lara Raj even appears uncredited with spoken parts on "Boys," which suggests a friendly connection, not a feud. I don't know in detail what she has said publicly about the Netflix "villain" edit. But the album fits her stated wish not to be polarizing for its own sake, and it puts her energy into proving herself rather than settling scores.
