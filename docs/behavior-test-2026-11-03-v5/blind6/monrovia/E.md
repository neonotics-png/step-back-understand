# Mon Rovîa / *Bloodline*: quiz answers (from memory)

**1. Core sound and the tension**
*Bloodline* is mostly acoustic and led by Mon Rovîa's ukulele and his soft, high, clear voice. Acoustic guitar, banjo, mandolin, fiddle and pedal steel back him up, and strings, piano, choirs and some synths add texture, so the overall sound is warm and gentle with occasional stomp-and-holler singalongs. Critics, notably Pitchfork and The Guardian, point to a clash between this soothing pastoral palette and graphic lyrics about war, violence and racism. "Running Boy" is the clearest case: a dangerous police encounter cuts into a warm singalong chorus, and Pitchfork called that clash "outright uncomfortable." "A Day at the Soccer Fields" pairs a calm sound with images of children shaped by violence in Liberia in a similar way.

**2. Sequencing as memoir**
It opens in war-time Liberia. "Black Cauldron" works as a prologue about inherited weight, followed by "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" (the women's peace movement) and "A Day at the Soccer Fields" (a return visit to Liberia), then the title track on ancestry. The middle moves through his American childhood and young adulthood: a childhood "foreshadowing," grief ("Little by Little"), Montana ("Old Fort Steele Trail"), adoption ("Whose Face Am I"), exhaustion ("Running Boy"), landscaping work ("Field Song") and the South ("Somewhere Down in Georgia"). It then turns outward and forward with "Oh Wide World," "Code of Many Colors," "Heavy Foot" and "Infinite Pines." It ends with "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea," a largely spoken-word epilogue that settles on peace and acceptance rather than answers.

**3. Spoken voices and found audio**
I don't know how "Whose Face Am I" uses spoken voices or found audio; my sources described it only as an adoption song about longing to know his unknown birth parents, so I won't invent details. What I can confirm is that the title track "Bloodline" reportedly uses found church sound under its choir and piano. The closer, "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea," is largely spoken word, and one reviewer found it superfluous even though it matters to the story. Both devices tie the search for origins to real voices and places rather than only to sung reflection.

**4. "Afro-Appalachian"**
He uses the term because the banjo, central to Appalachian music, descends from West African instruments, and Black musicians helped shape Appalachian folk before that history was largely erased. He told NME that learning the banjo came "from West Africa where I'm from" "really fortified" him. When he started out he saw few Black artists in folk and first made R&B instead, so the label lets a Liberian-born, Appalachia-raised Black artist claim folk as part of his own lineage rather than as borrowed ground. Some critics, such as VRT, argue the "Afro-" prefix is redundant for exactly that reason.

**5. From hip-hop to folk**
His early releases, *Sunburnt* (2020) and *Dark Continent* (2021), mixed hip-hop, trap, R&B and folk, partly because he saw few Black artists in folk and leaned toward R&B. Around 2022, after a period of Covid-era introspection, he turned to folk. A collaborator told him to drop the beats and just use his ukulele, and he took off on TikTok within weeks. On the personal side, he had tried to forget his early life in Liberia until survivor's guilt "caught up with him," and the bare, intimate ukulele-folk format suited the vulnerable storytelling that became the four *Act* EPs and then *Bloodline*.

**6. Traditions it draws on**
Liberian: "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" honors the Liberian women's peace movement led by Leymah Gbowee, in which Christian and Muslim women helped end the Second Civil War. The title turns Charles Taylor's boast that he could "pray the devil out of hell" back on him and borrows the name of the 2008 documentary. American: the album draws on Appalachian folk and bluegrass (the banjo on "Old Fort Steele Trail") and on the American protest-folk tradition. "Heavy Foot" is a protest song about a "heavy-footed government," and "Somewhere Down in Georgia" confronts the South's unreconciled racial history. "Field Song," built on claps and shouts, also nods to work-song traditions.

**7. Critical reception**
Reception was broadly positive. Pitchfork praised his "impressive clarity of vision," NME called it "purposeful, resonant folk storytelling," and Billboard called it "an anthem of strength and resistance." Atwood called it "breathtakingly beautiful," The Guardian compared him to Nick Drake and Labi Siffre, and NPR said it "doubly honors" his identity. Reservation one is the dissonance between soothing arrangements and graphic lyrics (Pitchfork on "Running Boy"), which shows how far the album commits to meeting violence with calm. Reservation two is that some songs feel generic or thin: Pitchfork found "Oh Wide World" generic and said "Heavy Foot" lacks substance on complex issues, which shows the risk of keeping hopeful, universal messages simple. Other reviewers questioned the "Afro-Appalachian"/"soulgrass" labeling and found the spoken-word closer superfluous.

**8. The "departure" framing**
The album is not only about Liberia. It is a memoir of his whole journey from Liberia to the American South, so Georgia and Tennessee are as much his story as Monrovia: he worked landscaping jobs in Georgia and was raised as a Black refugee child in a white household in the South. He would likely present "Somewhere Down in Georgia" as part of the same reckoning with history: "the past does not disappear when it goes unnamed," and as he said at the Tiny Desk, history should be told "the way it was." He would present "Heavy Foot" as a song of "joy and resistance," saying "together we're stronger" and "If I have mine and my freedom, I want to free other people as well." Both songs belong to the same thread that runs from the Liberian women's peace movement through American injustice.
