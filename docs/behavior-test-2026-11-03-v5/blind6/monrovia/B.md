# Quiz answers: Mon Rovîa and *Bloodline* (from memory, no new lookups)

**1. Core sound and its tension.** *Bloodline* is mostly acoustic. Ukulele and acoustic guitar lead, with banjo, mandolin, strings and fiddle, piano, upright bass and choir-like backing vocals around them. The overall texture is calm, warm and meditative. Critics, Pitchfork most clearly, point out that this soothing, pastoral sound carries graphic, traumatic lyrics: Pitchfork called the softness a "Trojan horse" and "a method of self-soothing." Pitchfork says the clash "gets outright uncomfortable" on "Running Boy," which turns a dangerous police encounter and survivor's guilt into a breezy singalong chorus. "Day at the Soccer Fields" sets violent childhood memories over sliding strings in a similar way.

**2. Sequencing as memoir.** The album starts at his origins in wartime Liberia. "Black Cauldron" covers his earliest sense of self, "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" honors the Liberian women's peace movement, and "Day at the Soccer Fields" covers a return to Liberia and children marked by war. The middle covers ancestry and displacement: the title track, "A Foreshadowing," "Little by Little" on grief, the Montana childhood of "Old Fort Steel Trail," the unknown parents of "Whose Face Am I," and the restless "Running Boy." After that come working life ("Field Song" from his landscaping years) and America's history and present ("Somewhere Down in Georgia," "Heavy Foot"). It ends with self-trust and peace: "Oh Wide World," "Code of Many Colors," "Infinite Pines," and the largely spoken-word closer "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea," which settles into peace rather than certainty.

**3. Spoken voices and found audio.** I don't know whether "Whose Face Am I" uses spoken voices or found audio. My sources described it only as a song of longing about not knowing his biological parents, a song about wanting to know your story, and I won't invent a device for it. The two places I can confirm are the title track "Bloodline," which reviewers said uses found church audio with piano and choir, and the closer "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea," which is mostly spoken word. At The Barrier found that spoken-word epilogue superfluous. In both, the device ties his search for roots to communal and church memory and to direct testimony, so the album works partly as a told story and not only as sung songs.

**4. Why "Afro-Appalachian."** He coined the term after learning that the banjo descends from West African instruments. He told NME it was "cool to learn about the instrument of the banjo coming from West Africa where I'm from" and that it "really fortified" him. The label joins his two homes, Liberia and the Tennessee foothills where he was raised. For a Black, Liberian-born artist who wasn't sure he would be accepted in a white-coded genre, it says he is reclaiming folk's under-documented African and African American history rather than borrowing from it. NPR described his sound as coming "full circle, doubly honoring his identity."

**5. From hip-hop to ukulele folk.** His early SoundCloud work, the 2020 EP *Sunburnt* and the 2021 album *Dark Continent* mixed hip-hop, trap, R&B and folk. He grew up on Kendrick Lamar and Lil Baby, and he has said he hesitated over folk partly because he doubted a Black artist would be accepted in it. The personal turn came through depression and survivor's guilt in college: journaling became songwriting, and his manager Eric Cromartie talked him out of quitting. On the career side, he picked up the ukulele around COVID ("COVID made the soil really ripe for singer-songwriters to come back"), moved to folk with "Vulture Culture" in 2022, and found that quiet ukulele songs took off on TikTok. That led to the hero's-journey EPs, the Nettwerk deal, and the intimate *Bloodline* sound. Learning about the banjo's West African roots gave him the confidence to claim the genre.

**6. Traditions it draws on.** On the Liberian side, "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" answers the history of the Liberian women's peace movement that helped end the civil war, the story of Leymah Gbowee told in the 2008 documentary of the same title. The shared title is my inference. On the American side, the album sits in Appalachian folk and bluegrass, and "Old Fort Steel Trail" is a clear example, with the exquisite banjo reviewers singled out. It also answers the American protest-song and civil-rights tradition: "Heavy Foot" is a stomp-along protest anthem that puts him in the TikTok folk-protest wave, and "Somewhere Down in Georgia" confronts the South's violent racial history. More loosely, reviewers hear the singer-songwriter lineage of Paul Simon, Cat Stevens and Nick Drake.

**7. Critical reception.** Reviews were broadly positive to rapturous. Pitchfork praised his "impressive clarity of vision," The Guardian praised the "arresting serenity" set against his harrowing past (comparing him to Nick Drake and Labi Siffre), Billboard called it "an anthem of strength and resistance," Atwood called it "breathtakingly beautiful," and NME put him on its cover. Reservation one, from Pitchfork: at 16 tracks the album sometimes "lapses into more generic imagery about overcoming fear," which shows the downside of an uplift-and-healing mission, where universal hope can blur the specific story. Reservation two, also from Pitchfork: the gentle pastoral sound against graphic content can grate, as on "Running Boy," which shows the risk of the "Trojan horse" approach of soothing the listener first. A smaller one came from At The Barrier, which called the spoken-word closer superfluous and suggested the memoir framing sometimes runs past what the music needs.

**8. Why "departure" misreads the record.** The album isn't only about Liberia. It traces his whole path from Liberia to the American South, and its title is about how inherited history lives in you. That includes the history of the country he grew up in as a Black refugee raised in a white family in Tennessee. "Somewhere Down in Georgia" is about living in the South and confronting unaddressed history ("The past does not disappear when it goes unnamed"). "Heavy Foot" is about the present: homelessness, prisons, gun violence, war framed against genocide, and propaganda that blames immigrants. He said "Heavy Foot" prepares the way for an album about war, displacement and migration. He would likely frame both songs as continuous with the Liberian material, the same reckoning with violence, displacement and survival seen from his American present. He would present them as songs of "joy and resistance" and unity rather than a detour, with facing the past honestly as the condition for healing.
