# Mon Rovîa / Bloodline quiz answers

1. **Core sound and tension.** The album rests on his light, high voice over acoustic strings: guitar and ukulele, with fiddle, mandolin, banjo, dobro, strings and some piano. I'm not certain whether guitar or ukulele leads on most tracks. Ukulele is how he started, but reviews mention it only briefly, and Pitchfork's summary stresses "soothing guitar and fiddle." The texture is warm, mellow and pastoral, and critics hear a deliberate clash between that soothing sound and graphic lyrics about war, guns and danger. On "Running Boy," a dangerous police encounter breaks into a sing-along chorus, and one reviewer called the effect "uncomfortable." On "Day at the Soccer Fields," sliding strings carry a childhood memory of a gun encounter.

2. **Sequencing as memoir.** It begins in wartime Liberia. "Black Cauldron" sets a Bible next to a rifle, and "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" calls up the Liberian women's peace movement. The middle covers displacement, arrival and the clash of cultures, with the identity search of "Whose Face Am I," "Day at the Soccer Fields" and the title track. It then moves to the American South and Appalachia: "Old Fort Steel Trail," "Field Song," "Somewhere Down in Georgia." It ends quietly with "Infinite Pines" and the spoken-word epilogue "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea," so it lands on hard-won belonging. Caveat: my sources disagreed on the exact track order, so this is the broad arc, not a verified tracklist.

3. **Spoken voice and found sound.** "Whose Face Am I" opens with spoken word in Liberian (African) English before the singing starts. That puts a voice from his birthplace into a song about a war child not knowing his parents' faces, so the place itself speaks rather than just being described. I don't know what the spoken passage actually says. The title track does something similar: it opens with recorded sound from a church service, which points to his missionary, church-raised upbringing. The album also closes on a spoken-word epilogue, "Where the Mountain Meets the Sea."

4. **Why "Afro-Appalachian."** The term joins his two homes, Liberia and the East Tennessee foothills. It also rests on the history that Appalachian music has West African roots: the banjo descends from West African instruments, and Black musicians mixed with Scots-Irish fiddle music in the mountains. That matters to him because he once thought folk was "a white experience." The framing lets him claim the genre as partly his own heritage rather than borrowed. He has said he wants young Black listeners to see they can tell their stories in folk.

5. **From rap to folk.** As a teen he listened mostly to Kendrick Lamar and Lil Baby, and his early releases, through the 2021 album *Dark Continent*, mixed hip-hop, trap and R&B. He says he felt pressure as a Black artist to make rap, and that people called his voice "unique" rather than "good." Folk was already in him (his brothers had played him Bon Iver and Fleet Foxes, and he calls Bon Iver his way into music). During COVID he picked up the ukulele, posted simple clips on TikTok at his manager Eric Cromartie's urging, and they spread fast. That success, plus his discovery of folk's West African roots and its storytelling depth, moved him toward folk from 2022 on.

6. **Traditions it draws on.** Liberian: "Pray the Devil Back to Hell" takes its title from the documentary about Leymah Gbowee and the Christian and Muslim women whose peace campaign helped end Liberia's second civil war. "Whose Face Am I" adds spoken Liberian English. American: "Old Fort Steel Trail" uses banjo, the instrument at the heart of the Afro-Appalachian story. "Field Song" uses claps, stomps, dobro and mandolin, and a reviewer heard chain-gang echoes turned into joy. Paul Simon's *Graceland* is a reference point that reviewers name, not one he names, on "Oh Wide World" and "Running Boy." "Code of Many Colors" may nod to Dolly Parton's "Coat of Many Colors," but that is my guess.

7. **Critical reception.** Praise was broad. Pitchfork said he approaches his upbringing "with an impressive clarity of vision," NME gave him a cover story, and the Guardian, NPR and Billboard all wrote favorably. One reviewer called the album a "confident and competent" and "multi-faceted" mellow record whose short vignettes are a strength. Reservations:
   - One reviewer called the spoken-word closer "somewhat superfluous." That suggests the memoir framing sometimes explains more than the songs need.
   - Pitchfork's summary called the soft-music/dark-lyric clash on "Running Boy" "uncomfortable." That is the cost of the gentle approach: it can feel at odds with the subject, even if that's the point.
   - One reviewer was wary of labels like "soulgrass," which shows the blend can be hard to place.

   I only found a few reviews in full, so this may miss other criticisms.

8. **The "departure" framing.** The album isn't only about Liberia. It is a memoir of both the Liberian roots and the American journey, and the Southern, Appalachian half is as much his "bloodline" as Monrovia is. The core Afro-Appalachian idea is that those two strands are connected. "Heavy Foot" is a protest song about poverty, gun violence, propaganda and government power. He said it "prepares the way" for *Bloodline* and that "it's about the moment we're living in," so he would call it part of the same mission, music as "both memoir and medicine," linking his survival to other people's struggles. About "Somewhere Down in Georgia" I only know the sound (rolling fingerpicked guitar, a change of meter, a triumphant middle section), not its lyrics. So I can't say exactly how he frames it, beyond that it belongs to the album's American chapter, not a detour from it.
