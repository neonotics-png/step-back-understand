# Understanding ADÉLA's *PRIMA* (2026)

*PRIMA* is a short, glossy pop debut (11 songs, about 31 minutes; Capitol Records; released 4 September 2026). It tells the story of a girl from Bratislava who trained like a ballerina to become an American pop star. It opens on ambition ("KGB"), works through sex, power and bad relationships in the middle, and closes on home and family ("Therapy", "Ain't In LA"). Most of what is odd or divisive about it comes from three things: who made it, which decade of pop it is aimed at, and where ADÉLA is looking at that pop from.

I haven't heard the album. Everything about how it sounds comes from reviews, credits and ADÉLA's own interviews.

## Why it sounds the way it does: two producers pulling in opposite directions

The executive producers are Dylan Brady and Blake Slatkin, and they produced nearly every track together. Brady is half of 100 gecs, the duo whose blown-out, genre-mashing "hyperpop" made distortion and excess into a style. He had already worked on ADÉLA's 2025 EP *The Provocateur*, which leaned industrial and hyperpop and had a Grimes co-production ("Machine Girl"). Slatkin is a mainstream hitmaker whose credits include Lizzo's "About Damn Time" and The Kid LAROI and Justin Bieber's "Stay": clean, radio-ready songs where the hook comes first.

*PRIMA* lands much closer to Slatkin than to the EP. Reviewers describe club and bass-heavy dance pop, a 90s-style horn chorus ("Fantasize"), and real piano ballads ("Hitachi", "Therapy"). Brady's influence comes through in the skittering, bass-flooded production on songs like "Nicole Kidman" and "Boys" rather than as full-on noise. Julia Michaels co-wrote most of the record, and that was ADÉLA's own choice. She told *Paper* that the EP was "me very much being like, this has to be me," and that its songs "are good, but they're not Julia Michaels level good pop songs." For the album she wanted to use "everybody's superpowers." She also said she is "kind of getting tired" of being provocative for its own sake.

That explains the split in reception. Fans of the gritty EP and some critics heard a step back into the "palatable." The Post (Ohio University) called it well made but less distinctive in the mainstream. Others heard a better-written record: *Variety* called it more memorable and more unusual than her earlier releases, and Clash gave it 8/10. If you know the EP, expect *PRIMA* to be tidier and more song-first. Expect less abrasion and more hooks.

## The decade it's aimed at: late-00s and early-2010s American pop

The record keeps reaching back to the pop of roughly 2007 to 2015. Critics hear Britney on "Boys", *Bangerz*-era Miley on "Ain't In LA", Ariana Grande in the hummed outro of "Hitachi", and 2015-style internet pop on "Marijuana". This is a deliberate choice, not a coincidence:

- **Who was in the room.** "Ain't In LA" was co-written with Theron Thomas, who co-wrote Miley Cyrus's "We Can't Stop" (2013). He supplies the slowed, chopped vocal intro, which is why that song sounds so much like 2013. "Nicole Kidman" was co-written with Uffie, the voice of the mid-2000s Ed Banger electroclash scene, whose cool, half-spoken sexiness fits that song.
- **The brief.** ADÉLA says she wanted it to be "the kind of album the girls from *Spring Breakers* would listen to." Rachel Sennott came up with the title.
- **Her own history.** She was born in 2003, learned English from American pop stars, and says *Hannah Montana* made her want to sing. So this is the pop of her childhood, heard from outside the US.

The downside is the most common criticism: at times the album "wears its influences too heavily on its sleeve" (The Post). Rolling Stone's review put it as "Predictable doesn't really work for her."

## The ballet frame: ambition as discipline

*PRIMA* means "first", since it's her debut, and it is also a nod to the prima ballerina, the top dancer in a company. ADÉLA says she wants to be "the prima ballerina of pop." That is literal background. She started ballet at three, danced the lead in *The Nutcracker* at the Slovak National Theatre as a child, and trained with the Vienna State Ballet and the English National Ballet School before COVID pushed her toward music. Her next stop was HYBE and Geffen's *Dream Academy*, a K-pop-style trainee competition, where she was the first contestant eliminated. The winners became KATSEYE.

Knowing this explains the opener. "KGB" uses the Soviet secret police as a metaphor for relentless training toward stardom. It's the logic of a ballet school and a trainee system, not a typical pop boast. It also explains the sense of hunger running through the record. The narrator on "Starving Artist" and the industry gatekeepers Rolling Stone noticed both come from someone who has been ranked and cut before. The same reviewer argued she is at her best "when she stops telling you she wants to be a big star", and that "KGB" tries too hard to sell what the rest of the album shows. KATSEYE's Lara Raj appears uncredited with spoken parts on "Boys", which nods to that shared beginning.

## What's its own: American pop seen from Bratislava

The sources above don't explain the album's emotional core, which is about distance. "Ain't In LA", the closer, was inspired by a photo of her mother at 18 in front of a wall of Western pop posters that ADÉLA's grandfather had smuggled into communist Czechoslovakia from Germany. The song is about a girl in hand-me-downs, with Madonna and Destiny's Child on her wall, insisting that "you don't have to be from a big city" to matter. ADÉLA shot the video in Bratislava but filled it with Hollywood imagery. "Therapy", a sparse, whispery piano ballad about her family and especially her father, is the record's rawest moment. Reviewers either found it the most affecting song or hard to sit through.

So *PRIMA* doesn't just copy 2010s American pop. It's that pop as an outsider fantasy, made by someone who has now reached LA and is singing about what it cost.

The other thing that is clearly hers is frankness, delivered, in Rolling Stone's words, "not with a wink, but with unwavering eye contact." "Hitachi" is a breakup ballad named after a vibrator. "Nicole Kidman" (written after she watched *Eyes Wide Shut* and felt like a movie star) borrows from *Moulin Rouge!* and *Babygirl* in its video. "I'm the Man" plays with gender roles. Several reviews single out her voice, especially on "Nicole Kidman" and "Hitachi", as the album's strongest asset.

## How it landed

- **Charts:** No. 4 on the Billboard 200 (about 56,000 units in week one) and No. 2 in the UK. It reached No. 1 in Slovakia and, according to Wikipedia's chart list, in the Netherlands, Belgium and Scotland.
- **Singles:** "Ain't In LA" made her the first Slovak artist on the Billboard Hot 100 (reported peaks differ between sources). "Nicole Kidman" became the bigger hit: No. 4 in the UK and her first US top 10, helped by Kidman's public support.
- **Critics:** Favorable but split, with a Metacritic score in the high 60s. *Variety* and Clash were near the top and Pitchfork was reportedly the most skeptical. The common view: a strong presence, a few standout songs ("Nicole Kidman", "Hitachi", "Red Bottoms", "Ain't In LA"), and a sound that is still more borrowed than fully her own.

The Post compared her to Olivia Rodrigo, whose debut *SOUR* also wore its influences openly before she settled into a signature sound. That's a fair way to think about *PRIMA*: a polished, confident first statement from someone whose most distinctive material comes from her own vantage point, not from the pop she grew up on.
