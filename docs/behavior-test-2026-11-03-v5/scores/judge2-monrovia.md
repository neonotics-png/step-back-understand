# Judge 2: Mon Rovîa / Bloodline

**Ranking: W > Z > Y > X**

All four answers read in full (W ~1430 words, X ~2950, Y ~3080, Z ~1460). No web checks; "wrong" below means internally inconsistent or contradicted by the other answers.

## 1. W
W gives the most understanding for the reading time. It explains why the music sounds the way it does: the foster brothers' Fleet Foxes/Bon Iver records, the lockdown ukulele, and the rap phase. It also explains why "Afro-Appalachian" is a historical claim, using the 1920s split into "hillbilly" and "race" record catalogues, which no other answer gives and which makes sense of his old belief that folk was "white." The Liberia–America reverse-route point, the hero's-journey EP arc and the protest strand fit together into one picture. W marks its inferences carefully (the spoken word as a remnant of his rap phase, the Campbell "return" reading, the title echoes) and flags the conflict over whether he was nearly a child soldier. Weaknesses: there is no track-by-track list, and it opens with "I haven't heard the record," which is honest but adds nothing. The "close to a billion" TikTok figure is label promo, and W doesn't say so.

## 2. Z
Z is close behind W and has a similar explanatory design: it states the album's central move (soft sound, hard material) up front and traces it back to his life and his route into folk. Its best parts are the concrete instrument list showing where "Afro-Appalachian" actually appears on the record, the contrast with Paul Simon as an outsider borrowing African sounds, and the "What is his own" section. Weaknesses: it says his version of the banjo history is "a little more romantic than the record supports" without saying how. It leans on single-source details (Tracy Chapman, Paul Simon as an influence), although it flags them, and it guesses that Cooper Holzman explains the "radio-ready" polish. It explains the historical erasure behind the label less well than W.

## 3. Y
Y is a thorough, well-organised reference dossier: a timeline, a track-by-track list, a reception table, and a short "Context worth knowing" section that does cover the banjo lineage, Liberia's founding and the EP cycle. It handles source conflicts openly (adoption details, the age, the Global Peace Warriors claim). It is the best answer if you want to look things up. But at about 3,000 words it is mostly facts with little explanation, and the "why" sits in a short closing section. Some details sit badly with the other answers: it calls "Heavy Foot" the lead single "around July 2025," while X has it in early September, and it puts the Mel Robbins appearance in 2026 where X says June 2025. It also gives the Tiny Desk band lineup, which is padding.

## 4. X
X is similar to Y in scope: a dossier with a timeline, track-by-track notes from FLOOD, and reception. Its closing "How to make sense of him" section is a useful but thin synthesis. Its main problem is that it credits production to Andre Samuel and Daylight from a single Native Instruments piece. The other three answers say the album was written and recorded in Los Angeles with Cooper Holzman, so this is probably wrong or about a different record, even though X labels it single-source. It also gives "No. 30 on Airplay" with no chart name, and its 2024 timeline packs TikTok stats into a row that is hard to read. It has the most reading time and the least explanation, and it carries the most likely factual error.

## Things that look wrong or overclaimed
- X: the Andre Samuel / Daylight production credit conflicts with the Cooper Holzman credit in W, Y and Z.
- X vs Y: they disagree on the "Heavy Foot" release timing (Sep 2025 vs about July 2025) and the year of the Mel Robbins podcast (2025 vs 2026). At least one of them is wrong.
- The "close to a billion TikTok views," "200M streams" and "Billboard chart debut" figures are label promo; only Y labels them as such.
- Z: the Holzman-explains-polish point and the "more romantic than the record supports" aside are unsupported, though Z flags the first as inference.
- W and Z: the "Coat of Many Colors" and *Pray the Devil Back to Hell* links are inferences, and both answers say so.
